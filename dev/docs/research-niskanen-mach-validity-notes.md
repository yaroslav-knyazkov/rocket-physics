# Research notes: Niskanen aerodynamic model — Mach-number validity range

## Кратко (RU)

Тезис Нисканена сам заявляет точность примерно до M=1.5 (сверхзвуковых лётных данных у автора не было вообще). Собственная документация/вики/исходники OpenRocket признают, что при больших сверхзвуковых числах Маха результат менее надёжен (завышенный Cd), официального численного порога предупреждения в коде по Mach нет (есть только качественный `Warning.SUPERSONIC`, срабатывающий просто при переходе M>1, без порога "ненадёжности"). По формулам: ни формула трения Cf, ни формула кромочного сопротивления фин CD_LE не имеют математической сингулярности в рабочем диапазоне: обе построены кусочно именно так, чтобы избежать особенности при M=1 (кусок "1-M²" в CD_LE обрывается на M=0.9, до полюса на M=1). У Cf остаётся не сингулярность, а скачок значения ~2% на стыке M=1 (дозвуковая и сверхзвуковая поправки не точно совпадают там, автор это не скрывает). Поэтому водораздел типа "M=0.9", "M=1" и "M=1.3" в тех реализациях, что их используют — это границы самого тезиса (явно поименованные там же: 0.9, 1, 1.3 для конуса в Appendix B), а не произвольный программный выбор. Найден независимый факт (баг в самом Java-коде OpenRocket, issue #2543): НЕ формула Нисканена, а упрощённая Prandtl-Glauert поправка `f/beta` в `TubeCalc.java` действительно взрывается при M→1 — это иллюстрирует, почему наивные реализации без защитного кусочного построения ловят сингулярность, но это не относится к собственно описанным в вопросе формулам Cf/CD_LE.

## Question 1: Mach range claimed/validated in Niskanen's thesis itself

**Source: Niskanen, "Development of an Open Source model rocket simulation software" (Master's thesis, Helsinki University of Technology, 2009), https://openrocket.sourceforge.net/thesis.pdf**

- Abstract (p. ii/iii, both Finnish and English versions): *"While access to flight data of supersonic rockets was unavailable, it is expected for the simulation to be reasonably accurate up to Mach 1.5."* This is the thesis's own explicit, numeric claim of validity range — and it is explicitly a prediction/expectation, not a validated result (no supersonic flight data was available to the author to check it against).
- §1.1 Objectives: *"The emphasis will be on subsonic flight, but extensions will be made for reasonable estimation at transonic and low supersonic velocities."* — confirms the stated design intent is subsonic-primary with only a "reasonable estimation" extension into low supersonic, not full supersonic coverage.
- §3.1.2 "Velocity regions" (p. 18-19, Table 3.1) defines: Subsonic 0–0.8, Transonic 0.8–1.2, Supersonic 1.2–~5, Hypersonic ~5–. It states explicitly: *"Hypersonic flight will not be considered, since practically no model or high power rockets ever achieve such speeds."* and *"Methods for predicting the aerodynamic properties of subsonic flight and some extensions to supersonic flight will be presented."*
- §3.4.3 on the Barrowman shock-expansion method for nose pressure drag: the thesis notes Barrowman's own supersonic method "cannot handle body areas that have a slope larger than approximately 30°" and "cannot handle transonic effects" — which is precisely why Niskanen replaces it with the semi-empirical Appendix B method (Stoney curves) instead of using a theoretically rigorous supersonic treatment.
- Conclusion (per the independent Rust reimplementation's citation, confirmed against techdoc wording): the thesis itself (p. 94 area) repeats the "reasonably accurate to at least Mach 1.5" expectation in the conclusions chapter.

**Answer to Q1:** the thesis itself extends the treatment nominally up to M≈5 in its equations (Appendix B's cone/Stoney formulas are defined and evaluated at e.g. M=1.3+ and beyond), but the thesis's own *claimed validity/confidence* statement is narrower: only "reasonably accurate... up to Mach 1.5", and that claim is admittedly untested (no supersonic flight data was available to the author). It is NOT documented as subsonic-only (the equations are deliberately built with transonic/low-supersonic branches), but it is also not claimed/validated as reliable all the way to M=2–3 — that range is mathematically evaluable (the formulas don't become undefined) but outside the thesis's own stated confidence band.

## Question 2: OpenRocket docs/source — stated max reliable Mach

- **OpenRocket wiki, "Third-Party Compatibility"** (https://wiki.openrocket.info/Third-Party_Compatibility): *"But, at higher supersonic speeds the OpenRocket simulation produces less reliable results, producing a too high drag coefficient. If a flight requires a high level of accuracy, RASAero uses flight projection equations equivalent to professional engineering aerodynamic analysis methods."* — this is a qualitative statement ("higher supersonic speeds"), **no specific numeric Mach threshold is given** in this official wiki page.
- **Community guidance echoing OpenRocket dev** (rocketryforum.com, 2012, user CarVac who is an OpenRocket contributor): *"OpenRocket doesn't trust itself to simulate completely accurately at supersonic speeds. However for most flights (less than Mach 2) it shouldn't be a problem, unless you need to predict altitudes for getting call-in waivers."* (https://www.rocketryforum.com/threads/openrocket-supersonic-flight-problems.46484/) — gives an informal "Mach 2" rule of thumb, but this is a forum post, not an official doc/source statement.
- **OpenRocket Java source** (`net.sf.openrocket.aerodynamics.Warning`, via javatips.net mirror of openrocket-master): there IS a dedicated `Warning.SUPERSONIC` constant, i.e. the software does surface a user-facing warning when a simulated flight goes supersonic. However the mirror snippet found does not show a specific *numeric Mach threshold* tied to a "results unreliable above M=X" message — the warning appears to be a binary "this flight went supersonic" flag rather than a graduated/thresholded reliability warning. (Could not confirm the exact trigger condition/value from this source listing alone — would need the live OpenRocket GitHub repo's `FlightConditions`/calculator code to pin the exact trigger, which was not fetched in this pass.)
- **Imperial College Rocketry wiki analysis of OpenRocket** (https://wiki.imperialrocketry.com/en/FlightDynamics/Analysis_of_OpenRocket), a third-party technical analysis, states under "Shortcomings": *"Due to the fact that interpolation is used for transonic flight, [there] is no way to tell if the simulations run by models that go past Mach 0.8 are legitimate..."* and *"CP location and drag coefficient estimated to an accuracy of 10% from the original value at subsonic velocities and deviates even further as the Mach number increases."* — again qualitative, no single hard numeric cutoff from OpenRocket itself.
- **Live, documented regression/bug in OpenRocket's own code** — GitHub issue openrocket/openrocket#2543 ("[Bug] Launch lug pressure CD is extremely high for Mach values very close, but over 1"), https://github.com/openrocket/openrocket/issues/2543: a maintainer traces the bug to `TubeCalc.java`'s Prandtl-Glauert-style correction `f = f / beta` where `beta = sqrt(|1 - M^2|)`, which blows up as M→1 from above. A discussant cites Ketchledge's "Active Guidance and Dynamic Flight Mechanics for Model Rockets" (1993) recommending the correction factor be clamped to 0.6 between M=0.8 and M=1.1. This is an **actual, acknowledged near-M=1 singularity bug in OpenRocket's shipped Java code** (not in Niskanen's thesis equations as published) — directly relevant to Q3's general concern, discussed there.

**Answer to Q2:** No single official numeric "beyond this Mach, results are unreliable" threshold was found stated as a hard documented constant in OpenRocket's own docs or source. The qualitative consensus across OpenRocket's own wiki, its developers (forum) and third-party analyses is: trustworthy through roughly M≈1.2–1.5 (matching the thesis's own M=1.5 claim), informally "probably fine below M≈2", and explicitly "less reliable"/"too high Cd" at "higher supersonic speeds" beyond that, with no stated upper bound where it is formally invalid — Niskanen's own math is evaluated by OpenRocket all the way to M=5 (hypersonic boundary per Table 3.1), it's just not claimed to be *accurate* there.

## Question 3: Hard mathematical restrictions (singularities) in Cf / fin CD_LE formulas

**Source for the exact equations: Niskanen thesis §3.4.2 (skin friction, eq. 3.77–3.85) and §3.4.4 (fin pressure drag, eq. 3.89–3.93), cross-checked against the fully-cited independent Rust reimplementation `hpr_aero` (https://hpr.fusionspace.co/api/src/hpr_aero/drag.rs.html, https://hpr.fusionspace.co/api/src/hpr_aero/nose_drag.rs.html), which documents each line against the specific thesis equation number.**

### 3a. Skin friction coefficient Cf (eq. 3.77–3.85)

- Base turbulent Cf (eq. 3.78): `Cf = 1/(1.50 ln R - 5.6)^2` — this *can* in principle blow up only if `1.50 ln R - 5.6 = 0`, i.e. R = e^(5.6/1.5) ≈ 44.6 — far below the thesis's stated low-Reynolds floor (eq. 3.81 clamps Cf to a constant 1.48e-2 for R<1e4), so the pole is never reached in the formula's actual domain of use. Not a practical issue.
- Compressibility correction (eq. 3.82–3.84):
  - Subsonic (M<1): `Cfc = Cf·(1 - 0.1·M²)` — a simple polynomial, defined and finite for all M, including M up to 3+ (though physically meaningless there since it's only meant for M<1; at M slightly under 1 it gives `0.9·Cf`).
  - Supersonic turbulent (M≥1): `Cfc = Cf/(1+0.15·M²)^0.58` — denominator is always ≥1, never zero or near-zero; defined and smoothly evaluable for all M≥1 through M=3 and beyond. No singularity.
  - Supersonic roughness-limited (M≥1): `Cfc = Cf/(1+0.18·M²)` — same, no singularity, denominator always ≥1.
- **Finding: there is NO mathematical singularity in the Cf formula or its compressibility corrections anywhere from M=0 through M=3 (or further).** All three branches are polynomials or simple rational functions in M with no zero crossings of their denominators in the relevant domain.
- **However, there IS a genuine discontinuity in *value* (not a blow-up, just a jump) exactly at the M=1 switch-over** between the subsonic and supersonic correction formulas: at M=1, the subsonic branch gives `0.900·Cf`, the supersonic turbulent branch gives `0.922·Cf`, and the supersonic roughness-limited branch gives `0.847·Cf` of the base `Cf`. The thesis's independent reimplementation documents this explicitly: *"The subsonic and supersonic corrections differ at M = 1 (0.900 against 0.922 of Cf turbulent, 0.847 roughness-limited), as published; no transonic blend is given."* (hpr_aero drag.rs doc comment, directly citing Niskanen eq. 3.82–3.84 and the underlying Barrowman 1967 eq. 4-12/4-13). This is a real, published small jump discontinuity (~2-6%) at M=1 in the raw formula, not smoothed by Niskanen; it is a minor data artifact inherited from Barrowman 1967, not a software choice, and not a singularity.

### 3b. Fin leading-edge pressure drag CD,LE (eq. 3.89, after Barrowman 1967 eq. 4-17–4-19)

Piecewise definition (confirmed directly from the thesis PDF text via Firecrawl/Exa, §3.4.4, eq. 3.89):
```
(CD•)_LE⊥ = (1 - M²)^(-0.417) - 1          for M < 0.9
          = 1 - 1.785·(M - 0.9)            for 0.9 < M < 1
          = 1.214 - 0.502/M² + 0.1095/M⁴   for M > 1
```
- The first branch, `(1-M²)^(-0.417)`, **does have a true mathematical singularity exactly at M=1** (denominator (1-M²)→0, exponent is negative so the value diverges to +∞ as M→1⁻). **But the thesis never evaluates that branch anywhere near M=1** — it switches to the linear middle branch at M=0.9, well before the pole, specifically to sidestep it.
- Checking continuity at the switch points (verified numerically from the formula itself):
  - At M=0.9: branch 1 gives `(1-0.81)^(-0.417) - 1 = 0.19^(-0.417) - 1 ≈ 1.999 - 1 = 0.999 ≈ 1`; branch 2 at M=0.9 gives `1 - 1.785·0 = 1`. These match (continuous, no jump).
  - At M=1: branch 2 gives `1 - 1.785·0.1 = 0.8215`; branch 3 at M=1 gives `1.214 - 0.502 + 0.1095 = 0.8215`. These also match exactly (continuous, no jump).
  - Branch 3 is a simple rational polynomial in 1/M², finite and smoothly evaluable for all M≥1, including through M=3.
- **Finding: the CD,LE formula is fully continuous (value-matched, by construction) across its entire 0 ≤ M ≤ 3+ domain, with the M=0.9 and M=1.0 breakpoints being genuine, deliberately-placed boundaries in the original equation itself (to avoid the M=1 pole of the first branch) — not an arbitrary implementation/plotting choice.** The independent reimplementation's source comments corroborate this reading verbatim (citing "eq. 3.89 (after Barrowman 1967 eq. 4-17–4-19)").

### 3c. The "M=0.7"/"M=1.3"-style segmentation seen in some implementations — thesis boundary or software choice?

Checked against Appendix B (nose/body wave drag, which the question also references):
- The thesis itself documents a **genuine M=1.3 boundary**, not invented by any implementation: Niskanen's Appendix B.2 for cone pressure drag states the closed-form supersonic formula (eq. B.4, `C(M) = 2.1·s² + 0.5·s/√(M²-1)`) applies "M ≳ 1.3" (page 107 of the thesis, per the Rust reimplementation's cited constant `CONE_SUPERSONIC_MACH = 1.3`), and between M=1 and M=1.3 the thesis prescribes "polynomial interpolation with the boundary conditions from equations (B.4), (B.5) and (B.6)" — i.e. a cubic (Hermite) blend matching both value and slope at M=1 and M=1.3. This M=1.3 breakpoint is explicitly Niskanen's own, stated in the thesis text, not a downstream implementation's arbitrary choice.
- Similarly, the M=0.8 boundary (start of the semi-empirical transonic/Appendix-B method, below which eq. 3.86/3.87 subsonic interpolation applies) is explicitly Niskanen's own choice, stated in §3.4.3: *"The semi-empirical method is used at Mach numbers above 0.8."*
- eq. B.4's own formula `C(M) = 2.1·s² + 0.5·s/√(M²-1)` has a term `1/√(M²-1)`, which **is mathematically singular exactly at M=1** — but again, the thesis never evaluates it there: it only applies from M=1.3 upward (and uses the Hermite cubic blend for 1 ≤ M < 1.3, which has no such singularity, being a pure cubic polynomial matching the endpoint slopes).
- **Conclusion on 3c:** the "M≈0.7–0.9" / "M=1.3" style segmentation that shows up in correct re-implementations is, wherever it was checked in this research pass, a faithful reproduction of boundaries stated explicitly in Niskanen's own thesis text (page-cited: M=0.8 at §3.4.3, M=0.9/1.0 at eq. 3.89, M=1/1.3 at Appendix B.2) — placed precisely to keep every formula's genuinely-singular term (the `(1-M²)^(-0.417)` pole at M=1 in eq. 3.89, and the `1/√(M²-1)` pole at M=1 in eq. B.4) safely out of the range where that branch is ever evaluated. It is not an arbitrary software/plotting choice as far as this research could determine, though an implementation is free to choose a *different* valid blending strategy between the same boundary values/slopes (e.g. the independent Rust port's own choice of a quadratic fallback fit for eq. 3.87 "where eq. 3.87 has no solution" is a genuine downstream implementation decision, distinct from the thesis's stated boundaries).

### 3d. Related but distinct finding: a real singularity bug in OpenRocket's actual Java code (not in the thesis as published)

Not one of the three formulas named in the question, but directly on-topic for "hard mathematical restriction / singularity" concerns: OpenRocket's shipped Java implementation (`TubeCalc.java`) has an **acknowledged, currently-open bug** where a Prandtl-Glauert-style correction `f = f / beta`, with `beta = sqrt(|1 - M²|)`, is applied to a body-tube pressure-drag term for M>1 — and `beta→0` as M→1, so the corrected CD "is extremely high for Mach values very close, but over 1." (GitHub issue openrocket/openrocket#2543, https://github.com/openrocket/openrocket/issues/2543, opened by SiboVG, discussion ongoing as of the issue thread; a proposed fix cites Ketchledge (1993) recommending clamping the correction factor to 0.6 between M=0.8 and M=1.1). This demonstrates concretely why *naively* applying a Prandtl-Glauert-type `1/β` compressibility factor without the kind of explicit piecewise/clamped construction Niskanen used for Cf and CD,LE produces a genuine blow-up near M=1 — reinforcing that the deliberate piecewise construction in eq. 3.82–3.84 and eq. 3.89 is specifically what avoids this failure mode, and that at least one *other* part of OpenRocket's codebase failed to apply the same discipline.
