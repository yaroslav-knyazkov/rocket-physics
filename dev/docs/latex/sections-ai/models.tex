\section{Models}\label{sec:models}
Each subsection gives the interchangeable models behind one term of the equations of motion: the
three forces (gravity, thrust with its mass, aerodynamics), the environment they depend on
(atmosphere, wind, relative flow), the Earth-rotation correction, and the integration. The last
four subsections --- launch rail, recovery, air brake and sampled-data mode --- modify the
equations rather than supply a term, and are off by default.

\subsection{Gravity}\label{sec:gravity}
This subsection supplies the weight $\vec{W}=mg\hat{z}_g$: the magnitude $g$ and the direction
$\hat{z}_g$ (down, along the local vertical), chosen independently. Below, $h$ is the altitude above
the surface and $R_e$ the Earth's mean radius.

\subsubsection{Magnitude}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Constant:} $g=g_0$.
    \item \textbf{Radial} (inverse-square, spherical non-rotating Earth).
    \item \textbf{Ellipsoidal} (WGS84, experimental): Somigliana surface gravity plus a
    second-order free-air correction, with $\phi$ the geodetic latitude of the launch site and
    constants from \autoref{sec:constants}.
\end{itemize}
\begin{gather}
    g(h)=g_0\left(\frac{R_e}{R_e+h}\right)^2 \qquad \text{(radial)} \\
    \gamma_0(\phi) = \gamma_e\frac{1+k\sin^2\phi}{\sqrt{1-e^2\sin^2\phi}} \\
    g(\phi, h) = \gamma_0\left[1 - \frac{2}{a_e}\left(1+f+m-2f\sin^2\phi\right)h + \frac{3}{a_e^2}h^2\right].
\end{gather}
The ellipsoidal magnitude already contains the centrifugal part of the Earth's rotation.

\subsubsection{Direction}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Parallel} (default): $\hat{z}_g\equiv\hat{z}_0$, the local vertical at the launch
    point, for the whole flight.
    \item \textbf{Radial:} $\hat{z}_g$ points to the centre $O$ of a sphere of radius $R_e$.
    \item \textbf{Ellipsoidal:} $\hat{z}_g$ is the inward normal of the WGS84 ellipsoid.
\end{itemize}
With $\vec{Og}=\vec{OO^*}+\vec{O^*g}$, $\vec{OO^*}=-R_e\hat{z}_0$ and $\vec{O^*g}=x\hat{x}_0+z\hat{z}_0$
in the pitch plane,
\begin{align}
    \text{parallel:}\quad & \hat{z}_g = \hat{z}_0, \\
    \text{radial:}\quad & \hat{z}_g = -\frac{\vec{Og}}{|\vec{Og}|}
        = -\sin\varphi\,\hat{x}_0 + \cos\varphi\,\hat{z}_0, \qquad \tan\varphi=\frac{x}{R_e-z}.
        \label{eq:zhat_g_rotated}
\end{align}
The code uses the arc angle $\varphi=x/R_e$ (\autoref{eq:phi_downrange}), which agrees with the
exact angle at $z=0$ and to first order elsewhere.

For the ellipsoid the normal does not pass through $O$: it makes the angle $\phi-\psi$ with $\vec{Og}$,
where $\psi$ is the geocentric latitude, $\tan\psi=(1-e^2)\tan\phi$ (at most $0.19^\circ$). The
normal at the rocket is the normal at $O^*$ turned by the change of geodetic latitude along the
track, $\vartheta=\int_0^x \mathrm{d}x'/R_n(x')$, with $R_n$ the local normal-curvature radius, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zhat_g_ellipsoid}
    \hat{z}_g = -\sin\vartheta\,\hat{x}_0 + \cos\vartheta\,\hat{z}_0 .
\end{equation}
The code freezes $R_n$ at its launch value, the radius of the osculating sphere in the flight
azimuth $\chi$ (Euler's formula, with $M,N$ the meridional and prime-vertical radii of curvature),
\begin{equation}
    R_\chi = \left(\frac{\cos^2\chi}{M(\phi_0)} + \frac{\sin^2\chi}{N(\phi_0)}\right)^{-1},
    \quad M = \frac{a_e(1-e^2)}{(1-e^2\sin^2\phi)^{3/2}}, \quad
    N = \frac{a_e}{\sqrt{1-e^2\sin^2\phi}},
\end{equation}
so $\vartheta=x/R_\chi$, i.e.\ \autoref{eq:zhat_g_rotated} with $R_e\to R_\chi$.
\todo{this differs from the exact normal at third order in $\vartheta$, because $R_n$ keeps
changing with latitude along the track.}

\paragraph{Position relative to $O$ and true altitude.} The non-parallel models need the altitude
$h_{true}$ over the curved ground, which the altitude-dependent models use in place of $-z$. The
arc angle and the ground point under the rocket are
\begin{gather}
    \varphi = \frac{x}{R_e}, \label{eq:phi_downrange}\\
    \vec{O^*P}(\varphi) = R_e\sin\varphi\,\hat{x}_0 + R_e(1-\cos\varphi)\,\hat{z}_0,
        \label{eq:surface_point}
\end{gather}
and the altitude is the offset of the rocket from that point along $-\hat{z}_g$,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:h_true}
    h_{true}(x,z) = \left[x\hat{x}_0+z\hat{z}_0-\vec{O^*P}(\varphi)\right]\cdot\bigl(-\hat{z}_g\bigr)
        = x\sin\varphi - z\cos\varphi - R_e(1-\cos\varphi).
\end{equation}
For small $\varphi$, $h_{true}\approx x^2/2R_e-z$. Impact is $h_{true}=0$ instead of $z=0$. For the
ellipsoid, $R_e\to R_\chi$.

A single model may supply $g$ and $\hat{z}_g$ together, as a vector field
$\vec{g}(\vec{x},t)$ from a gridded dataset, so that $\vec{W}=m\vec{g}$.

\subsection{Thrust}\label{sec:thrust}
\subsubsection{Magnitude}
Thrust comes from the momentum of the ejected propellant and the pressure difference at the
nozzle exit,
\begin{equation}
    T = \left|\dot m_m\right| v_e + A_e(p_e - p_{atm}),
\end{equation}
with $\dot m_m$ the motor mass flow, $v_e$ the exhaust speed, $p_e$ the exit pressure, $A_e$ the exit
area and $p_{atm}$ the ambient pressure. In practice a static-test curve is used instead. The
\emph{reference} thrust $T_0(t)$ is given as points $(t_i,T_i)$, $i=1,\dots,N$, from a motor file,
interpolated linearly, held at $T_1$ for $t<t_1$ and zero for $t>t_N$; burnout is its falling
zero-crossing:
\begin{equation}
    T_0(t) = \mathrm{lerp}\!\left(t; \{t_i\}, \{T_i\}\right).
\end{equation}
The test was run at a fixed ambient pressure $p_0$. While the motor is flowing ($T_0>0$), the
thrust in flight is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thrust_pressure}
    T(t, h) = T_0(t) + A_e\left(p_0 - p_{atm}(h)\right),
\end{equation}
which rises with altitude by at most $A_ep_0$ in vacuum; $A_e=0$ recovers $T_0(t)$ exactly.
\todo{sim/ names $p_0$ \texttt{p.thrust.p\_ref} and $A_e$ \texttt{p.thrust.A\_e}; the
default $A_e=0$ switches the correction off.}

\subsubsection{Direction}
The line of thrust may differ from the nose axis by a small angle $\theta_t$. Its unit vector
$\hat{x}_t$ follows from $\hat{x}_r$ exactly as $\hat{x}_r$ does from $\hat{x}_0$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thrust_frame}
    \hat{x}_t = \cos\theta_t\,\hat{x}_r - \sin\theta_t\,\hat{z}_r, \qquad
    \hat{z}_t = \sin\theta_t\,\hat{x}_r + \cos\theta_t\,\hat{z}_r, \qquad \hat{y}_t\equiv\hat{y}_r,
\end{equation}
so a positive $\theta_t$ tilts the thrust line the way a positive $\theta_r$ tilts the nose. Three
options:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ideal} (default): $\theta_t=0$.
    \item \textbf{Misalignment}: $\theta_t$ is a constant input, a manufacturing-type error.
    \item \textbf{Controlled orientation} (gimbal): $\theta_t(t)$ commanded by a control law, the
    only pitch authority once off the rail. Future work.
\end{itemize}
The axial component is $T\cos\theta_t$ ($4\times10^{-5}$ lost at $0.5^\circ$), while
$T\sin\theta_t$ acts at the arm $l_t-l_g$ and matters as a moment.

\subsection{Mass, centre of gravity and moment of inertia}\label{sec:mass}
As propellant burns, mass leaves through the nozzle and both the centre of gravity and the moment
of inertia change. The rocket is split into two bodies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the \emph{dry rocket} (subscript $r$), everything that does not burn: constant mass
    $m_r$, centre of gravity $l_{g,r}$ from the nose tip $A$, inertia $I_{r,g}$ about its own
    centre of gravity;
    \item the \emph{motor} (subscript $m$), casing and remaining propellant: mass $m_m(t)$, centre
    of gravity $l_{g,m}(t)$, inertia $I_{m,g}(t)$ about its own centre of gravity.
\end{itemize}
The subscript $g$ in $I_{r,g}$, $I_{m,g}$ means ``about that body's own centre of gravity''; the
totals $m$, $l_g$, $I$ carry no body subscript. $t$ is time since ignition. The subscript $p$ is not
used for the propellant, as it names the aerodynamic reference point. $g_0$ is the standard
gravity of the definition of specific impulse, unrelated to the local $g(h)$.

\subsubsection{Mass}\label{sec:mass_mass}
The total mass is the dry rocket plus the motor,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_total}
    m(t) = m_r + m_m(t).
\end{equation}
Only $m_m$ changes. It starts at $m_0$ and follows the mass flow until burnout, after which it is
held at $m_f$ (the casing and whatever else stays; $m_f=0$ if $m_m$ counts propellant only):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_motor}
    m_m(t) = m_0 + \int_0^t \dot m_m\,\mathrm{d}t', \qquad m_m\ge m_f.
\end{equation}
The mass flow $\dot m_m<0$ is the only thing the two motor mass models supply.

\paragraph{Linear model.} Inputs: $m_0$, $m_f$ and the burn duration $\Delta t$. The flow is
constant,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_linear}
    \dot m_m = \frac{m_f-m_0}{\Delta t} \quad(0\le t\le\Delta t),
\end{equation}
whatever the shape of the thrust curve.

\paragraph{Specific-impulse model.} Inputs: the thrust curve and the specific impulse
$\mathrm{ISP}$ (seconds). The propellant leaves at the rate that delivers exactly that thrust,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_isp}
    \dot m_m = -\frac{T_0(t)}{\mathrm{ISP}\;g_0},
\end{equation}
integrated until $m_m$ reaches $m_f$. It uses the \emph{reference} curve $T_0(t)$, not the
pressure-corrected $T$, because $\mathrm{ISP}$ is defined from the stand measurement; the exit
pressure term changes the force on the airframe, not the propellant consumption. When the model is
built from the thrust file alone, $\mathrm{ISP}$ follows from the total impulse $J=\int T_0\,
\mathrm{d}t$ and the burned mass $\Delta m=m_0-m_f$ as $\mathrm{ISP}=J/(\Delta m\,g_0)$, so that
\autoref{eq:mass_isp} burns exactly $\Delta m$.

\subsubsection{Centre of gravity}\label{sec:mass_cog}
The first moment of mass about the nose tip $A$ is additive:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_cog}
    l_g(t)\,m(t) = l_{g,r}\,m_r + l_{g,m}(t)\,m_m(t)
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    l_g(t) = \frac{l_{g,r}\,m_r + l_{g,m}(t)\,m_m(t)}{m(t)}.
\end{equation}
The same holds for a non-zero radial offset,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_cog_r}
    r_g(t)\,m(t) = r_{g,r}\,m_r + r_{g,m}(t)\,m_m(t).
\end{equation}
The simplest model of the motor's own centre of gravity is linear in the \emph{burned} mass
$m_b=m_0-m_m$, with $l_0$, $l_f$ its values at ignition and burnout and $\Delta m=m_0-m_f$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_l_linear}
    \frac{\partial l_{g,m}}{\partial m_b}=\frac{l_f-l_0}{\Delta m},\qquad
    l_{g,m}(m_m)=l_0+\frac{l_f-l_0}{\Delta m}\,(m_0-m_m).
\end{equation}
(With respect to $m_m$ itself the sign of the derivative would flip.)

\subsubsection{Moment of inertia}\label{sec:mass_inertia}
The inertias add once each body's own inertia is transferred to the total centre of gravity $g$
by the parallel-axis theorem,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_inertia}
    I(t) = I_r + I_m(t),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_inertia_parts}
    I_r = I_{r,g} + m_r\,d_r^2, \qquad I_m(t) = I_{m,g}(t) + m_m(t)\,d_m(t)^2,
\end{equation}
with the transfer distances in the pitch plane
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_dist}
    d_r^2=\bigl(l_g(t)-l_{g,r}\bigr)^2+\bigl(r_g(t)-r_{g,r}\bigr)^2, \qquad
    d_m^2=\bigl(l_g(t)-l_{g,m}(t)\bigr)^2+\bigl(r_g(t)-r_{g,m}(t)\bigr)^2.
\end{equation}
On the axis ($r\equiv0$) only the axial terms remain. $I_r$ depends on time through $l_g(t)$
although $I_{r,g}$ and $m_r$ do not.

The motor's own inertia is modelled like its centre of gravity, linear in burned mass, with $I_0$,
$I_f$ its values at ignition and burnout:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_I_linear}
    \frac{\partial I_{m,g}}{\partial m_b}=\frac{I_f-I_0}{\Delta m},\qquad
    I_{m,g}(m_m)=I_0+\frac{I_f-I_0}{\Delta m}\,(m_0-m_m).
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Propellant grain geometry}
The propellant part of $m_m(t)$, $l_{g,m}(t)$ and $I_{m,g}(t)$ depends on the grain geometry and
how it recedes. Solid-motor grains come in a few families: \textbf{cylindrical} (solid or bored,
e.g.\ BATES, burning on the bore and possibly the end faces); \textbf{tubular} (case-bonded, bore
only); \textbf{end-burning} (one flat face, nearly constant burn area and thrust, a centroid moving
quite differently); and \textbf{star or finocyl} (a shaped perforation chosen to tailor $T_0(t)$).
Each gives its own burn area against web thickness $A_b(w)$; with a regression rate
$\mathrm{d}w/\mathrm{d}t=r_b(p)$ (Saint-Robert's law, $r_b=ap^n$, $p$ the chamber pressure), the
propellant volume, centroid and inertia follow by integration over the receding grain, and with the
casing give $l_{g,m}(t)$ and $I_{m,g}(t)$.

The linear models and the fixed-centroid lump stand in for that information: they assume a
uniform density and a shape that merely shrinks, which no real grain does. The placeholder stays
until an actual grain geometry is chosen.

\subsection{Aerodynamics}\label{sec:aero}
\subsubsection{Force and moment}
The force $\vec{A}_f$ and moment $\vec{A}_m$ of \autoref{eq:aero_magnitudes} are \emph{definitions},
not a model: they hold for every coefficient model, and the force is never supplied directly. $S$
is the cross-section of the body tube and $c$ its diameter, which also forms the Reynolds number.
One pair $(S,c)$ per component is shared by all of its coefficient models, since a coefficient
means nothing except relative to the pair it was normalised by.

\paragraph{Multi-component aerodynamics.} When the airframe is built from several components $k$
(body, fins, nose cone, \dots), each with its own reference point $pp_k$, force $\vec{A}_{f,k}$ and
moment $\vec{A}_{m,k}$ about $pp_k$, the total force is the sum and the total moment about $p$
adds the moment of each force about $p$:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:aero_multi}
    \vec{A}_f = \sum_k \vec{A}_{f,k}, \qquad
    \vec{A}_m(p) = \sum_k \vec{A}_{m,k} + \sum_k \vec{p\,pp_k}\times\vec{A}_{f,k},
\end{equation}
with $\vec{p\,pp_k}$ the position of $pp_k$ relative to $p$. Each component is normalised by its
own reference area and length, $\vec{A}_{f,k}=-qS_k(C_{x,k}\hat{x}_r+C_{z,k}\hat{z}_r)$ and
$\vec{A}_{m,k}=-qS_kc_k\,C_{m,k}\hat{y}_r$, so $S_k,c_k$ generally differ from the airframe's $S,c$.
\todo{sim/ carries a single aerodynamic component (one $S,c$ and one coefficient set per airframe);
\autoref{eq:aero_multi} is not implemented.}

$l_p-l_g$ is the axial distance from $g$ to $p$, positive with $p$ aft, so that
$\vec{gp}=-(l_p-l_g)\hat{x}_r$ and the caliber static margin is $\mathrm{SM}=(l_p-l_g)/c$.

\paragraph{Axial force and the flow direction.} The term $-qSC_x\hat{x}_r$ assumes nose-first
flight with small $|\alpha|$. Since $C_x$ is positive, the term always points aft; flying
tail-first (after apogee on a ballistic descent, or in a tumble) it would \emph{accelerate} the
rocket. The physical axial force opposes the axial component of the relative flow, so its sign
should follow $\hat{x}_r\cdot\vec{v}_{rel}$, equivalently $\cos\alpha$.
\todo{The axial term assumes nose-first flight. The correction (a sign factor on
$C_x$ that follows $\cos\alpha$, continuous through $\alpha=90^\circ$ and equal to $1$ for
nose-first flight) is a separate code decision; once accepted, state the factor here.}

\subsubsection{Dynamic pressure}
\begin{equation}
    q = \tfrac12\rho v_{rel}^2,
\end{equation}
with $\rho$ the local air density and $v_{rel}$ the speed relative to the air. Both $v_{rel}$ and
the angle of attack $\alpha$ come from the relative-flow model.

\subsubsection{Coefficients}\label{sec:aero_coeffs}
$C_x,C_z,C_m$ and the arm $l_p-l_g$ are not derived from the airframe's shape; they are supplied.
Three interchangeable models supply them, selected per run. Writing $C$ for any of $C_x,C_z,C_m$,
in order of increasing fidelity: constant,
\begin{equation}
    C = C_0,
\end{equation}
linearized about a reference condition in the angle of attack, Mach number and Reynolds number,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:aero_general_linear}
    C(\alpha,M,Re) = C_0 + \alpha\frac{\partial C}{\partial\alpha} + M\frac{\partial C}{\partial M}
        + Re\frac{\partial C}{\partial Re},
\end{equation}
and real data, a multivariable lookup table $C(\alpha,M,Re)$. The form each takes in the code:
\todo{sim/ implements the $\alpha$ slope only in the linearized model (no $M$ or $Re$ slope), and
the table model is a two-variable $(M,\alpha)$ table; $Re$ enters neither.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Constant}: $C_x,C_z,C_m$ and $l_p-l_g$ are fixed inputs, independent of $\alpha$
    and $M$. Useful for first-cut sizing and for isolating one effect in a sweep. The forces still
    vary over the flight, since the coefficients are multiplied by $q$.
    \item \textbf{Linearized}: a single slope in $\alpha$ about zero $\alpha$, with the moment arm
    still fixed,
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:aero_linearized}
        C_x = C_{x,0}, \qquad C_z = C_{z,\alpha}\alpha, \qquad C_m = C_{m,\alpha}\alpha, \qquad l_p-l_g \text{ fixed}.
    \end{equation}
    This is \autoref{eq:aero_general_linear} with $C_{z,0}=C_{m,0}=0$, no $M$ or $Re$ slope, valid
    for small $\alpha$. In the code $C_z,C_m$ use $C_{z,\alpha}\sin\alpha$, $C_{m,\alpha}\sin\alpha$
    (within $0.4\%$ of the linear form at $8.6^\circ$). This is a robustness fix, not a different
    model: $\alpha=\operatorname{atan2}(w,u)$ jumps at its branch cut ($u<0$, $w$ crossing zero),
    which a rocket coasting tail-first through $v_{rel}=0$ at apogee lands on exactly, and the
    unbounded linear form turns that into a jump in the moment. $\sin\alpha$ removes it and is
    exactly zero at $\alpha=\pi$, as the net side force of a body of revolution is.
    \item \textbf{Table}: $C_x,C_z,C_m$ and $l_p-l_g$ are read from a database populated once per
    airframe (wind tunnel, CFD, or a semi-empirical tool) and interpolated at run time, in
    practice over a rectangular $(M,\alpha)$ grid: $M$ is bracketed and clamped at the grid edges,
    $\alpha$ is interpolated linearly within each bracketing row, and the two rows are blended
    linearly. This is the only model in which $l_p-l_g$, and hence the static margin, responds to
    the flight state.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Table file format.} The table is a CSV file \texttt{data/aero/<name>.csv}, read by
\texttt{sim/aero.py}. Lines starting with \texttt{\#} and blank lines are comments; the first other
line is the header, and every following line is one grid point $(M,\alpha)$:
\begin{center}

\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
\textbf{Column} & \textbf{Meaning} & \textbf{Units} \\
\hline
\texttt{alpha\_deg} & angle of attack $\alpha$ (axis) & deg \\
\texttt{M} & Mach number (axis) & --- \\
\texttt{C\_A} & axial-force coefficient ($C_x$) & --- \\
\texttt{C\_N} & normal-force coefficient ($C_z$) & --- \\

\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Pitch damping across the models.} The pitch-damping derivative $C_{m,q}$ is a property
of the airframe and applies under all three models; positive $C_{m,q}$ is damping in this
report's sign of $\vec{A}_m$. Constant and Linearized take it as a single constant; Table takes it
from the optional column and falls back to that constant. It adds the term $\hat{\omega}\,C_{m,q}$
to $C_m$, with $\hat{\omega}=\omega_r c/(2v_{rel})$ the non-dimensional pitch rate, evaluated in the
product form
\begin{equation} \label{eq:pitch_damping_moment}
    qSc\,\hat{\omega}\,C_{m,q} = \tfrac14\rho v_{rel}Sc^2\,\omega_r\,C_{m,q},
\end{equation}
which never divides by $v_{rel}$, zero at apogee.

\subsubsection{Linearization of coefficients}\label{sec:aero_linearization}
For the static-stability analysis the coefficients are linearised in $\alpha$,
\begin{equation}
    C_z = C_{z,0} + \alpha\,C_{z,\alpha}, \qquad
    C_m = C_{m,0} + \alpha\,C_{m,\alpha},
\end{equation}
with slopes $C_{z,\alpha}=\partial C_z/\partial\alpha$, $C_{m,\alpha}=\partial C_m/\partial\alpha$
and offsets $C_{z,0}$, $C_{m,0}$. This is an analysis device applied to whichever coefficients are
in force; the \textbf{Linearized} model (\autoref{eq:aero_linearized}) is the case
$C_{z,0}=C_{m,0}=0$.

\paragraph{Optional drag polar.} Instead of an independent family, $C_x$ can be tied to the
normal-force coefficient through an induced-drag term,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:drag_polar}
    C_x = C_{x,0} + k\,C_z^2,
\end{equation}
with $C_{x,0}$ the zero-lift axial coefficient and $k$ the induced-drag factor. For a wing
$k=1/(e\pi\lambda)$, with $e$ the Oswald efficiency and $\lambda$ the aspect ratio; for a finned
body of revolution $k$ would be fitted to CFD or wind-tunnel data. The benefit is one coefficient
family less to tabulate and an axial force consistent with the normal force. The limits: it holds
for attached flow, is parabolic in $C_z$, misses flow separation and the Mach dependence of $k$
unless $C_{x,0}$ and $k$ are tabulated in $M$, and the axial force on a slender body at $\alpha$ is
not purely induced drag.

\subsection{Atmosphere}\label{sec:atmosphere}
Goal: given altitude $h$ (and, for the interpolated model, horizontal position), provide the local
air density $\rho$, speed of sound $a$ and dynamic viscosity $\mu$, which form the Reynolds and
Mach numbers.

\subsubsection{Model tiers}\label{sec:atmos_tiers}
Three tiers, increasing in fidelity, all exposing the same $(\rho, T, p, a, \mu)$ interface:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{ISA} (International Standard Atmosphere): piecewise-linear temperature lapse
    (troposphere to lower stratosphere), pressure from the hydrostatic equation, density from the
    ideal gas law, $a=\sqrt{\gamma R_{specific}T}$, viscosity from Sutherland's law.
    \item \textbf{Real data} (one sounding): a measured $(T(h), p(h))$ profile at one location and
    time, or an average for that location, interpolated in altitude only.
    \item \textbf{Real data across multiple stations}: the same from several locations,
    interpolated in altitude and horizontal position, and optionally time, along the ground
    track. The spatial interpolation is implemented (\emph{Field} below); \todo{interpolation in
    time between soundings is not implemented -- each station contributes one profile.}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Disturbances and gusts.} The tiers give the \emph{mean} state of the air. Turbulence and
gusts perturb its velocity, not its thermodynamic state, so they belong to the wind model. A
\emph{discrete gust} is a single deterministic event of prescribed shape (e.g.\ ``1-minus-cosine'');
\emph{continuous turbulence} is a stationary random process described by a spectrum. Only the
latter is modelled (Dryden). \todo{A discrete-gust model is not in the code; add it as a
deterministic wind increment $\Delta\vec{v}_w(t)$ if gust-response cases are wanted.}

\paragraph{ISA.} Geopotential altitude from the geometric altitude above mean sea level $h_{msl}$:
\begin{equation}
    H = \frac{R_{isa}h_{msl}}{R_{isa}+h_{msl}}, \qquad R_{isa} = 6356766\,\mathrm{m}.
\end{equation}
Seven layers, with base geopotential altitude $H_b$, lapse rate $L$, base temperature and pressure
$T_b, p_b$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l c c c c}
Layer & $H_b$ [m] & $L$ [K/m] & $T_b$ [K] & $p_b$ [Pa] \\
troposphere  & 0     & $-0.0065$ & 288.15 & 101325   \\
tropopause   & 11000 & $0$       & 216.65 & 22632.06 \\
stratosphere & 20000 & $+0.0010$ & 216.65 & 5474.889 \\
stratosphere & 32000 & $+0.0028$ & 228.65 & 868.0187 \\
stratopause  & 47000 & $0$       & 270.65 & 110.9063 \\
mesosphere   & 51000 & $-0.0028$ & 270.65 & 66.93887 \\
mesosphere   & 71000 & $-0.0020$ & 214.65 & 3.956420 \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Within the layer containing $H$, temperature is linear in geopotential altitude:
\begin{equation}
    T = T_b + L(H-H_b).
\end{equation}
For a non-zero lapse rate the pressure follows the polytropic relation,
\begin{equation}
    p = p_b(T/T_b)^{-g_{isa}/(LR_d)},
\end{equation}
and for an isothermal layer ($L=0$) the exponential form,
\begin{equation}
    p = p_b\exp\!\left(\frac{-g_{isa}(H-H_b)}{R_dT_b}\right).
\end{equation}
Density, speed of sound and viscosity follow from the ideal gas law and Sutherland's law:
\begin{equation}
    \rho = \frac{p}{R_dT}, \qquad a = \sqrt{\gamma R_dT}, \qquad
    \mu = \mu_0\left(\frac{T}{T_0}\right)^{3/2}\frac{T_0+S_\mu}{T+S_\mu},
\end{equation}
with $R_d = 287.0528\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$, $\gamma = 1.4$, $g_{isa} = 9.80665\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ (the
ISA's own fixed value), $\mu_0 = 1.716\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$,
$T_0 = 273.15\,\mathrm{K}$, $S_\mu = 110.4\,\mathrm{K}$.

\paragraph{Sounding.} One station's measured $(h_{msl}, p, T, u, v)$ profile, with temperature and
wind interpolated linearly and pressure log-linearly:
\begin{equation}
    T(h) = \mathrm{lerp}(h;\{h_i\},\{T_i\}), \quad
    p(h) = \exp\mathrm{lerp}(h;\{h_i\},\{\ln p_i\}), \quad
    u(h),v(h) = \mathrm{lerp}(h;\{h_i\},\{u_i,v_i\}).
\end{equation}
$\rho, a, \mu$ are derived from the interpolated $T, p$ as for ISA, never interpolated directly, to
stay thermodynamically consistent. Below the lowest level the profile continues with the
sounding's own lapse rate; above the highest, an ISA-shaped profile re-anchored at
$(h_{top}, T_{top}, p_{top})$ keeps $T, p$ continuous. Wind is held at the edge value outside the
data range.

\paragraph{Field.} $N$ stations are projected onto a tangent plane centred at the launch site
$(\phi_0,\lambda_0)$, together with the rocket's ground position $(x\sin\chi, x\cos\chi)$ ($x$ the
downrange component of $\vec{x}_r$, $\chi$ the launch heading, degrees clockwise from North, the
direction of $+\hat{x}_0$):
\begin{equation}
    E = R_e\cos\phi_0\,(\lambda-\lambda_0), \qquad N = R_e\,(\phi-\phi_0).
\end{equation}
The $N$ profiles are combined, weighted by inverse squared distance $d_i$, into one profile:
\begin{equation}
    w_i = \frac{1}{d_i^2+\varepsilon^2}, \qquad \hat{w}_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_j w_j}, \qquad
    (T, \ln p, u, v) = \sum_i \hat{w}_i\,(T_i, \ln p_i, u_i, v_i),
\end{equation}
then interpolated in altitude as in the sounding tier. A single station reduces every
$\hat{w}_i$ to 1 and reproduces the sounding tier exactly.

Inverse-distance weighting is the default because it is defined \emph{everywhere}, including far
outside the polygon the stations span, which a ground track routinely leaves; it reproduces a
station's own profile at that station, works with one or two stations as with $N$, and never
oscillates. $\varepsilon$ (a few hundred metres) removes the singularity at zero distance. A
thin-plate-spline radial basis function over the same coordinates is available as an alternative:
smoother with many well-spread stations, but it can overshoot and needs at least three
non-collinear stations. Methods defined only inside the convex hull of the stations are not
offered, as they return nothing at exactly the downrange distances of interest.

\subsubsection{Density}\label{sec:density}
Water vapour is lighter than dry air ($M_v=18.02$ against $M_d=28.96\,\mathrm{g/mol}$), so at a
given pressure and temperature damp air is \emph{less} dense. The effect is at the percent level,
and radiosondes report the dewpoint needed to correct for it.

\paragraph{Partial pressures.} Treat the mixture as two ideal gases sharing a volume, dry air at
partial pressure $p-e$ and vapour at $e$:
\begin{equation}
    \rho = \frac{p-e}{R_dT} + \frac{e}{R_vT}
         = \frac{p}{R_dT}\left[1 - \frac{e}{p}\left(1 - \epsilon\right)\right], \qquad
    \epsilon = \frac{R_d}{R_v} = \frac{287.0528}{461.5} = 0.6220.
\end{equation}
The bracket is always below one. Folding it into an effective \emph{virtual temperature}, the
temperature dry air would need to have the moist density at the same pressure, keeps the
dry-air gas constant $R_d$ in every formula:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:virtual_temperature}
    T_v = \frac{T}{1 - \dfrac{e}{p}\left(1-\epsilon\right)}, \qquad
    \rho = \frac{p}{R_dT_v},
\end{equation}
with $T_v \geq T$, equality for dry air.

\paragraph{Vapour pressure from dewpoint.} The dewpoint $T_d$ is the temperature to which the
parcel must be cooled, at constant pressure and mixing ratio, to reach saturation, so the vapour
pressure is the saturation pressure at the dewpoint, $e=e_s(T_d)$. For $e_s$ the Magnus form is
used, with the Alduchov--Eskridge coefficients, $T_d$ in $^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $e_s$ in hPa:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:magnus}
    e_s(T_d) = 6.1094\,\exp\!\left(\frac{17.625\,T_d}{T_d + 243.04}\right).
\end{equation}
It is accurate to better than $0.4\%$ over $-40$ to $+50\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. The factor of 100
converting hPa to Pa before forming $e/p$ is the easy mistake.

\paragraph{Scope: measured tiers only.} The correction is an opt-in switch, off by default (dry
air, $\rho=p/(R_dT)$). When on, it applies to the \textbf{sounding} and \textbf{field} tiers, not
to ISA, which is defined as dry air of fixed composition. A sounding without dewpoint data falls
back to $\rho=p/(R_dT)$ level by level.

\paragraph{Magnitude, and what is left dry.} At a warm, humid surface ($T=30\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$,
$T_d=26\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $p=101325\,\mathrm{Pa}$) the density correction is $-1.25\%$; at a
temperate surface ($T=14.2\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $T_d=10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$) $-0.48\%$; at a cold, dry
one ($T_d=-33\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$) $-0.017\%$; near the tropopause it is below $10^{-5}$, because
$e_s$ collapses with temperature. It is a boundary-layer effect, but that is where dynamic pressure
is largest early in the ascent. The speed of sound and viscosity are deliberately not corrected: a
consistent moist $a$ needs the mixture's own $\gamma$ as well as $R$, the two effects partly
cancel, and the remaining $\sim0.2\%$ is far below the uncertainty of the Mach-dependent
coefficients. The hydrostatic interpolation of $p$ between levels likewise stays on dry $T$; it is
a within-layer interpolation of measured endpoints, not an integration over a deep column.

\subsubsection{Reynolds and Mach numbers}\label{sec:reynolds_mach}
\begin{equation}
    Re = \frac{\rho v_{rel} c}{\mu}, \qquad M = \frac{v_{rel}}{a},
\end{equation}
with $v_{rel}$ the airspeed, $c$ the reference length (the body diameter), $\mu$ the dynamic
viscosity and $a$ the local speed of sound.

\subsection{Wind}\label{sec:wind}
Goal: given time $t$ and position (altitude $h$, and horizontal position for the interpolated
tier), provide the wind velocity $\vec{v}_w$ in $\mathcal{F}_0$. The aerodynamic force and moment
see the air only through $\vec{v}_{rel} = \vec{v}_r - \vec{v}_w$. The tiers mirror the atmosphere:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Simple analytic model}: constant wind plus an altitude-dependent shear profile,
    with an optional, separately selected turbulence term.
    \item \textbf{Real data} (one sounding): a measured or forecast profile $\vec{v}_w(h)$ at one
    location and time, or an average for that location.
    \item \textbf{Real data across multiple stations}: profiles from several locations,
    interpolated in altitude and horizontal position by inverse-distance weighting, as for the
    atmosphere (\texttt{sim/atmosphere.py}).
    \todo{Interpolation in time between soundings is not implemented.}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Flight-plane projection.} The launch heading $\chi$ fixes the 2D flight plane. A
horizontal wind given as ENU components $(u,v)$, or as meteorological speed and direction
$(W,\psi)$ with $u=-W\sin\psi,\ v=-W\cos\psi$ ($\psi$ the direction the wind blows \emph{from}),
projects onto it as
\begin{equation}
    w_x = u\sin\chi + v\cos\chi = -W\cos(\psi-\chi), \qquad
    w_y = u\cos\chi - v\sin\chi = -W\sin(\psi-\chi),
\end{equation}
with $w_x$ the downrange component feeding $\vec{v}_w$ and $w_z=0$. The cross-plane component
$w_y$ is computed and discarded: crosswind weathercocking needs a full 6DOF model.

\paragraph{Analytic.} Power-law shear with constant direction:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:wind_shear}
    W(h) = W_{ref}\left(\max(h,h_{ref})/h_{ref}\right)^{n}, \qquad \psi = \psi_0.
\end{equation}
$h$ is the altitude above the local ground and $n\approx1/7$ the shear exponent (open terrain;
lower for smoother, up to $\sim0.4$ for rougher terrain). It is valid within the surface layer only
--- the lowest few hundred metres to 1--2\,km --- above which the sounding tiers apply. This is the
\emph{mean} profile; the fluctuation about it is a separate model.

\paragraph{Sounding / field.} The same measured $(u,v)$ profiles and interpolation as the
atmosphere, projected through the formula above; never interpolated as $(W,\psi)$, since direction
wraps at $360^\circ$.

\paragraph{Turbulence.} A fluctuation added to the analytic mean profile, selected independently.
Two variants: \textbf{none} (default, exactly zero) and \textbf{Dryden}, the
MIL-F-8785C/MIL-HDBK-1797 horizontal turbulence spectrum. It applies to the analytic tier only; a
sounding is already a measurement of the real field.

Scale length and intensity come from the spec's low-altitude formulas, written in feet, so
$h_{ft}=h/0.3048$ is formed once, with $h$ the altitude above ground clamped at zero, and the band
logic stays in feet:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dryden_scales}
    L_u = L_v = \frac{h_{ft}}{\left(0.177+0.000823\,h_{ft}\right)^{1.2}}\ \mathrm{[ft]}, \qquad
    \sigma_u = \sigma_v = \frac{0.1\,W_{20}}{\left(0.177+0.000823\,h_{ft}\right)^{0.4}},
\end{equation}
with $W_{20}$ the mean profile \autoref{eq:wind_shear} at $20\,\mathrm{ft}$, so a run cannot have two
mean winds. $\sigma$ needs no unit conversion; $L$ does, on the way out. Three bands:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l l l l}
Band & Condition & $L_u=L_v$ & $\sigma_u=\sigma_v$ \\
low        & $h_{ft}\leq1000$      & \autoref{eq:dryden_scales} & \autoref{eq:dryden_scales} \\
transition & $1000<h_{ft}<2000$    & linear, $1000\to1750\,\mathrm{ft}$ & linear, $0.1W_{20}\to\sigma_{high}$ \\
isotropic  & $h_{ft}\geq2000$      & $1750\,\mathrm{ft}$ & $\sigma_{high}$ (an input) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The low formula is exact at its upper edge, so the transition band is continuous at both ends.
That band is an implementor's fill: the spec leaves the gap between $1000$ and $2000\,\mathrm{ft}$
undefined, and the two \emph{parameters} $(L,\sigma)$ are interpolated across it, not the
amplitudes, which go as $\sqrt{\Phi}$. $L$ and $\sigma$ have a slope kink at each boundary, like
the ISA lapse rates in $T(h)$; it is not smoothed, since no source specifies a smoothing.

The one-sided temporal power spectral densities are
\begin{gather}
    \Phi_u(\omega) = \sigma_u^2\,\frac{2L_u}{\pi v_{ref}}\,\frac{1}{1+\left(L_u\omega/v_{ref}\right)^2}
        \label{eq:dryden_psd_u} \\
    \Phi_v(\omega) = \sigma_v^2\,\frac{L_v}{\pi v_{ref}}\,
        \frac{1+3\left(L_v\omega/v_{ref}\right)^2}{\left[1+\left(L_v\omega/v_{ref}\right)^2\right]^2}
        \label{eq:dryden_psd_v}
\end{gather}
normalised so that $\int_0^\infty\Phi\,\mathrm{d}\omega=\sigma^2$. They are realised as a frozen
random-phase sum over a log-spaced frequency grid $\{\omega_i\}$ with bin widths
$\{\Delta\omega_i\}$ (periods from $600\,\mathrm{s}$, longer than any flight, down to
$0.2\,\mathrm{s}$, which the break frequency $v_{ref}/L_u$ demands just off the pad):
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dryden_synthesis}
    k_i = \Phi(\omega_i)\Delta\omega_i, \qquad
    w_i = \sqrt{\frac{2k_i}{\sum_jk_j}}, \qquad
    u(t) = \sigma_u\sum_i w_i\sin(\omega_it+\varphi_i),
\end{equation}
and likewise for $v(t)$ with independent phases. The textbook amplitude is $\sqrt{2\Phi_i
\Delta\omega_i}$; the normalisation by $\sum_jk_j$ gives $\sum_iw_i^2=2$ and hence
$\mathrm{Var}[u]=\sigma_u^2$ \emph{exactly} for any band and grid, instead of the $81$--$98\%$ a
finite band captures. So $\sigma$, the quantity the spec specifies, stays the physical knob. The
grid is deterministic and the phases $\{\varphi_i\}$ are drawn once per seed and frozen.

The components are \emph{along} the mean wind ($u$) and horizontally \emph{across} it ($v$), so
they compose with the flight-plane projection rather than adding to $W$ as scalars. With
$\hat{e}_u$ the unit vector along the mean flow, $\hat{e}_v$ it rotated by $+90^\circ$ in the
horizontal plane, and $\hat{y}_0$ the cross-plane axis,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dryden_composition}
    \hat{e}_u = -\cos(\psi-\chi)\,\hat{x}_0 - \sin(\psi-\chi)\,\hat{y}_0, \qquad
    \hat{e}_v = \sin(\psi-\chi)\,\hat{x}_0 - \cos(\psi-\chi)\,\hat{y}_0,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dryden_wind}
    (w_x,w_y) = (W+u)\,\hat{e}_u + v\,\hat{e}_v,
\end{equation}
which reduces to the mean-only projection when $u=v=0$. There is no non-negativity clamp on $W+u$:
a negative value is the gust reversing the local flow, whereas clamping a perturbed \emph{speed}
would silently flip the direction. Since the 2D closure discards $w_y$, $v$ has no effect on any
trajectory here; it is carried for a later 6DOF model.

Two limitations. First, $v_{ref}$ is a \emph{fixed input}, not the rocket's own $v_{rel}$. Dryden's
spectra describe a vehicle at constant airspeed in a frozen field, and this rocket's airspeed spans
zero on the pad to supersonic. Driving the spectrum with the instantaneous airspeed would make the
right-hand side depend on more than $(t,\vec{y})$, destroying the adaptive solver's step control
and reproducibility, since it re-evaluates at arbitrary, repeated and rejected times. After the
renormalisation of \autoref{eq:dryden_synthesis} the prefactors cancel, so $v_{ref}$ and $L$ enter
only through $L/v_{ref}$, the eddy-crossing time: $v_{ref}$ does not change the delivered intensity,
only where the spectrum's knee sits in frequency. Set it to the airspeed over the part of the flight
whose turbulence matters. Lifting this needs the sampled evaluation mode and a filter formulation.
Second, there is no vertical component, as for the mean profile. At $h=0$ the low formula gives
$L=0$ and the spectrum has zero energy while $\sigma$ stays finite; the perturbation is taken as
exactly zero there.

\subsubsection{Vertical wind}\label{sec:vertical_wind}
All tiers return horizontal wind only ($w_z=0$). The structure admits a vertical component:
$\vec{v}_w=w_x\hat{x}_0+w_z\hat{z}_0$, so an updraft of speed $w_{up}$ is $w_z=-w_{up}$ and changes
$\vec{v}_{rel}$, hence $v_{rel}$ and $\alpha$, directly. It matters most where $v_{rel}$ is small
(near the pad, around apogee, under canopy), since it rotates the relative wind by about
$w_{up}/v_{rel}$. Two families exist for deriving it: (1) terrain-slope orographic lift,
$w\approx\vec{v}_h\cdot\nabla h_{terrain}$, which needs a terrain elevation model; (2) thermal
updraft parameterisations from the soaring literature, which need surface heat-flux or insolation
data. Neither fits the current data pipeline without a new data source, so this is left
unimplemented pending research.

\subsection{Relative wind and angle of attack}\label{sec:relwind}
Let $\vec{v}_w$ be the wind velocity relative to $\mathcal{F}_0$ (a model output). The rocket's
velocity relative to the air is
\begin{equation}
    \vec{v}_{rel} = \vec{v}_r - \vec{v}_w = v_{rel,x}\hat{x}_r + v_{rel,z}\hat{z}_r,
\end{equation}
with $v_{rel,x}$ the axial and $v_{rel,z}$ the normal component in $\mathcal{F}_r$. The airspeed and
angle of attack are
\begin{equation}
    v_{rel} = \sqrt{v_{rel,x}^2+v_{rel,z}^2}, \qquad \alpha = \operatorname{atan2}(v_{rel,z}, v_{rel,x}),
\end{equation}
so $v_{rel,x}=v_{rel}\cos\alpha$, $v_{rel,z}=v_{rel}\sin\alpha$, and $\vec{v}_{rel}=v_{rel}\hat{x}_r$
at $\alpha=0$. The unit vector along the relative wind and its in-plane normal are
\begin{equation}
    \hat{u} = \frac{\vec{v}_{rel}}{v_{rel}} =\cos\alpha\,\hat{x}_r + \sin\alpha\,\hat{z}_r, \qquad
    \hat{n} = -\sin\alpha\,\hat{x}_r + \cos\alpha\,\hat{z}_r.
\end{equation}
They feed $q$ and, through $\alpha$, the coefficient lookup; the aerodynamic force itself is
resolved along the fixed body axes, not along $\hat{u},\hat{n}$.

\subsection{Non-inertial frame corrections (Earth's rotation)}\label{sec:noninertial}
The WGS84 gravity magnitude already contains the centrifugal effect of the Earth's rotation at the
launch site. Reinterpreting $\mathcal{F}_0$ as Earth-fixed adds two fictitious accelerations to the
translational equation:
\begin{equation}
    \vec{a}_{Coriolis} = -2\vec{\Omega}\times\vec{v}_r, \qquad
    \vec{a}_{centrifugal} = -\vec{\Omega}\times(\vec{\Omega}\times\vec{r}),
\end{equation}
with $\vec{\Omega}$ the Earth's angular velocity ($|\vec{\Omega}|=7.2921\times10^{-5}\,
\mathrm{rad/s}$) and $\vec{r},\vec{v}_r$ the rocket's position and velocity in the Earth-fixed
frame. The Coriolis term depends on the flight's own velocity and heading, and is the one
implemented.

\paragraph{Ground-frame basis.} Work in the local East--North--Up triad $(\hat{E},\hat{N},\hat{U})$
at the launch site $(\phi_0,\lambda_0)$, with $\phi_0$ the geodetic latitude (written $\phi$ in the
gravity model). $\mathcal{F}_0$ is fixed by the launch heading $\chi$ (degrees clockwise from
North, the direction of $+\hat{x}_0$) and $\hat{z}_0$ pointing \emph{down}:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{x}_0 = \sin\chi\,\hat{E} + \cos\chi\,\hat{N}, \qquad
    \hat{y}_0 = \cos\chi\,\hat{E} - \sin\chi\,\hat{N}, \qquad
    \hat{z}_0 = -\hat{U}.
\end{equation}
The triad is right-handed, $\hat{x}_0\times\hat{y}_0=\hat{z}_0$, and $\hat{y}_0$ points to the
\emph{right} of the downrange direction. The decomposition of $\vec{\Omega}$ below is evaluated
once, at the launch site, in this fixed frame; it does \emph{not} follow a rotating $\hat{z}_g$.

\paragraph{Components of $\vec{\Omega}$.} The rotation axis points to the celestial north pole,
which at latitude $\phi_0$ lies in the local meridian plane, tilted from the vertical by the
co-latitude:
\begin{equation}
    \vec{\Omega} = \Omega\left(\cos\phi_0\,\hat{N} + \sin\phi_0\,\hat{U}\right), \qquad
    \Omega = 7.2921\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{rad/s}.
\end{equation}
Projecting onto the basis vectors,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:omega_components}
    \vec{\Omega} = \Omega\left(\cos\phi_0\cos\chi\,\hat{x}_0
        - \cos\phi_0\sin\chi\,\hat{y}_0 - \sin\phi_0\,\hat{z}_0\right).
\end{equation}
Checks: at the North pole it is $-\Omega\hat{z}_0$ (straight up) for every $\chi$; on the equator
launching due North it is $+\Omega\hat{x}_0$; launching due East it is $-\Omega\hat{y}_0$, to the
left of downrange, which is North again. The negative sign on $\hat{z}_0$ is a consequence of the
$z$-down convention.

\paragraph{Which component matters in 2D.} With $\vec{v}_r=v_x\hat{x}_0+v_z\hat{z}_0$,
\begin{equation}
    \vec{\Omega}\times\vec{v}_r =
        \Omega_yv_z\,\hat{x}_0
        + \left(\Omega_zv_x - \Omega_xv_z\right)\hat{y}_0
        - \Omega_yv_x\,\hat{z}_0,
\end{equation}
so
\begin{equation} \label{eq:coriolis_split}
    \vec{a}_{Coriolis} = -2\vec{\Omega}\times\vec{v}_r =
        \underbrace{-2\Omega_y\left(v_z\hat{x}_0 - v_x\hat{z}_0\right)}_{\text{in-plane}}
        \;\underbrace{-\,2\left(\Omega_zv_x - \Omega_xv_z\right)\hat{y}_0}_{\text{out-of-plane}}.
\end{equation}
The in-plane acceleration comes \emph{entirely} from $\Omega_y$, the component normal to the
flight plane; $\Omega_x$ and $\Omega_z$, lying inside the plane, give a purely out-of-plane
acceleration. Projecting $\vec{\Omega}$ onto the plane would keep exactly the wrong half.

\paragraph{Simplification (2D closure).} Only the in-plane bracket is retained. This is not a
smallness argument: the discarded lateral acceleration is of the same order, and for some
geometries larger. It is dropped because the model has no lateral degree of freedom, the same
closure as for the crosswind $w_y$, lifted by the same future 6DOF work. Consequently
$\Omega_y=-\Omega\cos\phi_0\sin\chi$ vanishes for a launch due North or South and at the poles,
where the 2D model predicts \emph{zero} Coriolis effect although the true effect is maximal and
lateral. Substituting $\Omega_y$,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:coriolis_2d}
    \vec{a}_{Coriolis}^{\,2D} = 2\Omega\cos\phi_0\sin\chi\left(v_z\,\hat{x}_0 - v_x\,\hat{z}_0\right),
\end{equation}
that is, with $v_z<0$ for an ascending rocket,
\begin{equation}
    a_{Coriolis,x} = +2\Omega\cos\phi_0\sin\chi\;v_z, \qquad
    a_{Coriolis,z} = -2\Omega\cos\phi_0\sin\chi\;v_x.
\end{equation}
Two classical checks: a rocket rising vertically at the equator with $\hat{x}_0$ East ($\phi_0=0$,
$\chi=90^\circ$) gets $a_x<0$ and drifts \emph{West}; a body moving East ($v_x>0$) gets $a_z<0$,
upward --- the E\"otv\"os effect.

\paragraph{Residual centrifugal term.} Write the geocentric position as $\vec{r}=\vec{R}_0+\vec{s}$,
with $\vec{R}_0$ from the Earth's centre to the launch site and $\vec{s}=x\hat{x}_0+z\hat{z}_0$ the
simulation's position. Since $-\vec{\Omega}\times(\vec{\Omega}\times\vec{a})=\Omega^2\vec{a}-
(\vec{\Omega}\cdot\vec{a})\vec{\Omega}$ is linear in $\vec{a}$,
\begin{equation}
    \vec{a}_{centrifugal} = \underbrace{\Omega^2\vec{R}_0-(\vec{\Omega}\cdot\vec{R}_0)\vec{\Omega}}
        _{\text{constant, already in }\gamma_0(\phi)}
        + \underbrace{\Omega^2\vec{s}-(\vec{\Omega}\cdot\vec{s})\vec{\Omega}}_{\text{residual}}.
\end{equation}
The residual is bounded by $\Omega^2|\vec{s}|=5.3\times10^{-9}\,|\vec{s}|$, i.e.\
$5\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ at $|\vec{s}|=10\,\mathrm{km}$, three orders below the Coriolis
term, and is left unimplemented.

\paragraph{Moment.} Both accelerations are body forces proportional to mass, so their resultant acts
at $g$ and gives \emph{zero} moment about it. The only coupling is a gyroscopic torque from the
body's pitch rate, of order $2I\Omega\omega_r$, i.e.\ about $1.5\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}$ at
$\omega_r=1\,\mathrm{rad/s}$; it is dropped.

\paragraph{Result.} The translational equation gains one term, evaluated directly in
$\mathcal{F}_0$ like $\vec{W}$:
\begin{equation}
    m\vec{a}_r = \vec{W} +\vec{T} + \vec{A}_f + m\,\vec{a}_{Coriolis}^{\,2D}.
\end{equation}
It adds no parameter beyond $\phi_0$ and $\chi$. At $|\vec{v}_r|=300\,\mathrm{m/s}$ it is at most
$4.4\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ (equatorial East launch), about $2.8\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$
at $\phi_0=50^\circ$: $0.3\%$ of $g$, or order $10\,\mathrm{m}$ of displacement over a $30\,
\mathrm{s}$ powered ascent. This is the same order as the $g\,x/R_e\approx1.5\times10^{-2}\,
\mathrm{m/s^2}$ (at $x=10\,\mathrm{km}$) that gravity acquires along the rotated vertical; the two
are \emph{independent} and must not be multiplied together.

\paragraph{One residual inconsistency.} $\vec{\Omega}$ is resolved once at the launch site, in
the fixed frame $\mathcal{F}_0$, while gravity, if the radial or ellipsoidal direction model is
on, is resolved along the rotated $\hat{z}_g$. A single consistently rotating frame would need a
curvilinear formulation the 2D state cannot carry. The cost is small: $\Omega_y$ drifts by
$O(\Omega\,x/R_e)$, a relative $3\times10^{-6}$ of the Coriolis acceleration at $x=10\,\mathrm{km}$
and $3\times10^{-4}$ at $100\,\mathrm{km}$.

\subsection{States, initial conditions and integration}\label{sec:states}
The rocket has two groups of kinematic states, resolved in the fixed frame $\mathcal{F}_0$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{translation}: position $\vec{x}_r=x\,\hat{x}_0+z\,\hat{z}_0$, velocity
    $\vec{v}_r=v_x\,\hat{x}_0+v_z\,\hat{z}_0$ and acceleration $\vec{a}_r$;
    \item \emph{orientation}: pitch angle $\theta_r$, pitch rate $\omega_r$ and angular acceleration
    $\varepsilon_r$.
\end{itemize}
Each level is the time derivative of the one below it, and the mass follows the motor mass flow:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:state_kinematics}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}\vec{x}_r}{\mathrm{d}t}=\vec{v}_r, \quad
    \frac{\mathrm{d}\vec{v}_r}{\mathrm{d}t}=\vec{a}_r, \qquad
    \frac{\mathrm{d}\theta_r}{\mathrm{d}t}=\omega_r, \quad
    \frac{\mathrm{d}\omega_r}{\mathrm{d}t}=\varepsilon_r, \qquad
    \dot m=\dot m_m=-\frac{T_0(t)}{\mathrm{ISP}\,g_0}.
\end{equation}
The accelerations $\vec{a}_r$, $\varepsilon_r$ are outputs of \autoref{eq:eom_trans} and
\autoref{eq:eom_rot}, evaluated at every call of the right-hand side, not states. The integrated
state vector has dimension seven,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:state_vector}
    \vec{y} = \left(x,\ z,\ v_x,\ v_z,\ \theta_r,\ \omega_r,\ m\right),
\end{equation}
with $z$ and $v_z$ positive \emph{down}. $m$ is read only when the variable-mass model is on;
otherwise $m$ and $I$ are fixed and $\dot m=0$. The right-hand side has three modes, selected in
this order: on the rail, under canopy, otherwise free flight.
\todo{The code evaluates the mass flow with the thrust magnitude $T$ (including the nozzle-exit
pressure term), not the reference thrust $T_0$; the two differ only when the exit area is non-zero.}

\paragraph{Initial conditions.} The default start is at rest on the pad, at ignition, with the nose
along the rail:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:initial_conditions}
    \vec{x}_r(0)=\vec{0}, \quad \vec{v}_r(0)=\vec{0}, \quad
    \theta_r(0)=\theta_{rail}, \quad \omega_r(0)=0, \quad m(0)=m_{launch},
\end{equation}
with $\theta_{rail}$ the rail elevation (default $90^\circ$) and $m_{launch}=m_r+m_0$ the liftoff
mass. Time starts at $t=0$, the ignition instant of the thrust curve. The launch site supplies
\begin{itemize}
    \item latitude $\phi_0$ and heading $\chi$ --- Earth-rotation correction and ellipsoidal gravity;
    \item elevation $h_0$ --- atmosphere and gravity only; it never enters $z$;
    \item rail elevation and length --- the launch rail.
\end{itemize}
A scenario may override the whole state; nothing checks that $\theta_r(0)=\theta_{rail}$. The
models carry no hidden dynamical state, so they are pure functions of $(t,\vec{y})$.

\paragraph{Integrators.} Two integrators are available behind one interface, chosen by a setting:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adaptive Runge--Kutta 4(5)} (default): the \textsc{scipy} \texttt{RK45} scheme
    with relative tolerance $10^{-8}$ and absolute tolerance $10^{-10}$ on every component of
    $\vec{y}$, dense output on, and a time limit of $300\,\mathrm{s}$. Events are located to solver
    tolerance by root finding on the dense output.
    \item \textbf{Fixed-step classical Runge--Kutta 4}, hand-written, with step $h$ (default
    $1\,\mathrm{ms}$):
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:rk4}
        \vec{y}_{k+1}=\vec{y}_k+\frac{h}{6}\left(\vec{k}_1+2\vec{k}_2+2\vec{k}_3+\vec{k}_4\right),
    \end{equation}
    with $\vec{k}_1=\vec{f}(t_k,\vec{y}_k)$, $\vec{k}_{2,3}=\vec{f}\!\left(t_k+\tfrac{h}{2},
    \vec{y}_k+\tfrac{h}{2}\vec{k}_{1,2}\right)$, $\vec{k}_4=\vec{f}(t_k+h,
    \vec{y}_k+h\,\vec{k}_3)$ and $\vec{f}$ the right-hand side. It has no step control; events are
    detected afterwards from sign changes between steps and placed by linear interpolation, so they
    are accurate to about $h$. It is the cross-check of the adaptive path and the only one that
    supports the sampled-data mode.
\end{itemize}
Both integrate the same right-hand side and differ only in numerical error.
\todo{Document the cost/accuracy trade-off between the two integrators with a convergence
study (error against $h$ and against tolerance).}

\paragraph{Events.} Five events are watched; only the first ends the run. Each is a sign change of
a scalar function of $(t,\vec{y})$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ground impact} (terminal): the ground depth crosses zero going down, $z$ for flat
    ground, the curved-ground depth otherwise. Ignored for the first $0.1\,\mathrm{s}$, since
    $z=0$ at the start;
    \item \textbf{apogee}: $v_z$ crosses from negative to positive in the launch frame, also
    ignored for the first $0.1\,\mathrm{s}$;
    \item \textbf{burnout}: the thrust falls through a small threshold ($10^{-9}$\,N) from above,
    so a motor that never produces thrust never triggers it;
    \item \textbf{rail exit}: $s_{rail}$ crosses $L_{rail}$ upwards; never active when
    $L_{rail}=0$;
    \item \textbf{parachute deployment}: the deployment predicate turns true; never active without
    a recovery model.
\end{itemize}
Apart from ground impact the events only report times. The rail, canopy and air-brake modes are
state predicates evaluated inside the right-hand side, which cannot read an event not yet
finalised; hence the same condition appears twice, as a predicate that acts and an event that
reports.
\todo{The apogee event is the launch-frame $v_z$ crossing, not the maximum of the true altitude
above a curved ground; redefining apogee is a separate modelling decision.}

\subsection{Launch rail}\label{sec:rail}
On the launch rail the rocket can only slide along it: the rail buttons react every force component
transverse to the rail and prevent rotation. This is a \emph{kinematic constraint}, not a new
force: the assembled acceleration is projected onto the rail, and the rotation is removed.

The rail direction is fixed and is the direction the nose initially points. With $\theta_{rail}$
the rail elevation, an input set consistently with the initial $\theta_r$,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:rail_direction}
    \hat{e}_{rail} = \cos\theta_{rail}\,\hat{x}_0 - \sin\theta_{rail}\,\hat{z}_0,
\end{equation}
that is, $\hat{x}_r$ at $\theta_r=\theta_{rail}$, frozen. The distance travelled from a launch
point at the origin is the projection of the position onto it,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:rail_distance}
    s_{rail}(\vec{x}_r) = \vec{x}_r\cdot\hat{e}_{rail}
        = x\cos\theta_{rail}-z\sin\theta_{rail}.
\end{equation}
The rocket is on the rail while $s_{rail}<L_{rail}$ (a state predicate), and there the equations
of motion are replaced by
\begin{gather}
    \vec{a}_r = \left[\left(\frac{\sum\vec{F}}{m}
        + \vec{a}_{Coriolis}^{\,2D}\right)\cdot\hat{e}_{rail}\right]\hat{e}_{rail}
        \label{eq:rail_constraint} \\
    \omega_r = 0, \qquad \varepsilon_r = 0 \label{eq:rail_attitude}
\end{gather}
with $\sum\vec{F}$ the sum of \autoref{eq:eom_trans} and $\vec{a}_{Coriolis}^{\,2D}$ the term of
\autoref{eq:coriolis_2d} if enabled. The component of $\vec{a}_r$ normal to the rail is identically
zero, and since $\vec{x}_r(0)$ lies on the rail line with $\vec{v}_r(0)=\vec{0}$, position and
velocity stay on it with no correction step. In the integrator, \autoref{eq:rail_attitude} reads
$\mathrm{d}\theta_r/\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{d}\omega_r/\mathrm{d}t=0$, so $\theta_r$ and $\omega_r$ keep
their initial values. The pitching moment is reacted by the rail rather than allowed to act.

\paragraph{Exit and reverse motion.} $L_{rail}=0$ is the default and the off switch. Past rail
exit ($s_{rail}\geq L_{rail}$) the free-flight equations apply again, with no transition term.
Nothing holds the rocket on the pad before thrust exceeds weight.
\todo{No pad contact: if the thrust along the rail is below the weight component, \autoref{eq:rail_constraint}
gives a negative $\vec{a}_r\cdot\hat{e}_{rail}$ and the rocket slides \emph{backwards} down the
rail ($s_{rail}<0$) until thrust catches up. A real pad reacts weight until lift-off; the fix is to
clamp $\vec{a}_r\cdot\hat{e}_{rail}\geq0$ while $s_{rail}\leq0$ and $\vec{v}_r\cdot\hat{e}_{rail}\leq0$,
which would change existing results for low thrust-to-weight starts.}
The key output is the rail-exit speed $|\vec{v}_r|$, which decides whether wind can tip the
airframe before it can weathercock. There is no rail friction, flex or tip-off: the transverse and
rotational freedoms are restored instantly at $s_{rail}=L_{rail}$.

\subsection{Recovery}\label{sec:recovery}
Once a canopy is deployed the attitude is frozen exactly as on the rail, $\omega_r=0$,
$\varepsilon_r=0$, and only the translational equation changes. Deployment is instantaneous and
total, and the canopy is modelled by its drag alone:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:chute_eom}
    m\vec{a}_r = \vec{W} + \vec{T} + \vec{A}_f + \vec{F}_{ch},
\end{equation}
with $\vec{F}_{ch}$ the canopy drag below. The orientation is held at the pitch angle at
deployment, and the pitching moment is not integrated. Freezing is chosen over an artificial
damping term, which would need a time constant no source fixes.
\todo{In the code only $\mathrm{d}\theta_r/\mathrm{d}t$ and $\mathrm{d}\omega_r/\mathrm{d}t$ are set
to zero, so the stored $\omega_r$ keeps its non-zero value at deployment and the link
$\omega_r=\mathrm{d}\theta_r/\mathrm{d}t$ is broken under canopy.
The reported pitch rate is then meaningless after deployment; consider resetting it to zero.}

\paragraph{Deployment.} A function of the state, not of a detected event: either a timer,
$t\geq t_{dep}$, or apogee, taken as $v_z>0$ (descending, $z$ being positive down). The latter is
reconstructed from the state because the right-hand side cannot consult an event not yet
finalised, so a pre-apogee transient in $v_z$ --- possible on an unstable trajectory --- would arm
deployment early. This is not guarded against. These are the only two triggers; the predicate is
not latched, but both are monotone on a nominal flight. While the rocket is on the rail the rail
mode takes precedence.
\todo{No altitude (or velocity/dynamic-pressure) deployment trigger exists; a main-chute-at-altitude
trigger and a drogue-plus-main sequence are not modelled.}

\paragraph{Force.} The canopy drag is computed directly in $\mathcal{F}_0$, like weight and unlike
the body-axis aerodynamic force:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:chute_force}
    \vec{F}_{ch} = -\tfrac12\rho\,C_{D,ch}S_{ch}\,v_{rel}\,\vec{v}_{rel},
\end{equation}
with $S_{ch}$ the canopy reference area and $C_{D,ch}$ its drag coefficient. Three properties are
the whole content of the model:
\begin{itemize}
    \item It is \emph{natively inertial}, not routed through the body axial/normal decomposition. A
    round canopy is axisymmetric and its drag opposes the relative-wind \emph{vector}, not the nose
    direction, which under canopy is frozen at whatever attitude deployment left. Routing it through
    $\hat{x}_r$ would make the descent rate depend on that attitude. $C_{D,ch}$ is therefore a drag
    coefficient on $\hat{v}_{rel}$, not the axial $C_x$, with its own reference area $S_{ch}$.
    \item It is \emph{added to} the airframe's forces: weight, any residual thrust and the body-axis
    aerodynamic force all continue to act, evaluated at the frozen $\theta_r$.
    \item It is written as $v_{rel}\,\vec{v}_{rel}$ rather than $\tfrac12\rho v_{rel}^2C_{D,ch}S_{ch}\,
    \hat{v}_{rel}$, so it has no $0/0$ direction at $v_{rel}=0$.
\end{itemize}
A run can be checked against the terminal descent speed, where $\vec{F}_{ch}$ balances weight,
approached rather than attained since $\rho$ varies:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:chute_terminal}
    v_{term} = \sqrt{\frac{2mg}{\rho\,C_{D,ch}S_{ch}}}.
\end{equation}
$S_{ch}=0$ is the default, as is ``no recovery model''; either alone leaves this subsection inert.
Inflation is instantaneous and total, with no opening transient, shock or reefing, and the frozen
attitude replaces the pendulum swing under the risers.
\todo{Neglected and not yet in the model: the opening transient (drag area
growing from zero over an inflation time), the opening-shock load on the airframe and shock cord, a
reefed or two-stage (drogue and main) sequence, the pendulum and coning motion of the body under the
risers, and the added-mass effect of the air carried with the canopy.}

\subsection{Air brake}\label{sec:airbrake}
A switchable extra drag area, rigidly mounted to the body, used to modulate drag during the
unpowered coast. It has its own area and drag coefficient, not derived from an airframe, and enters
as one additive axial term, so that $qSC_x$ becomes $qSC_x+F_b$ with
\begin{equation} \label{eq:airbrake_force}
    F_b = q\,C_{D,b}\,S_b\,f_b, \qquad f_b\in[0,1],
\end{equation}
where $S_b$ is the exposed area at full deployment, $C_{D,b}$ its drag coefficient and $f_b$ the
deployment fraction: a linear fraction-to-area model, $S(f_b)=f_bS_b$. The term acts along
$\hat{x}_r$ whatever the pitch, and only on the axial force: nothing is added to $qSC_z$ or
$qSc\,C_m$, and the arm $l_p-l_g$ does not move. $S_b=0$ is the default.

Two orthogonal choices set $f_b$: a \emph{law}, which decides how far open the brake is, and a
\emph{trigger}, which decides from when it may open.

\subsubsection{Deployment law}\label{sec:airbrake_law}
Three interchangeable variants:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{None} (default): $f_b\equiv0$.
    \item \textbf{Proportional}: P control on \emph{predicted} apogee,
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:airbrake_p}
        f_b = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(k_p\left(\hat{h}_a-h_{tgt}\right),\,0,\,1\right), \qquad
        \hat{h}_a = h + \frac{v_z^2}{2g},
    \end{equation}
    with $h_{tgt}$ the commanded apogee and $k_p$ the gain. $\hat{h}_a$ is the altitude the rocket
    would reach if drag vanished now, deliberately an \emph{upper} bound that ignores all further
    drag, including the brake's own. A positive error (predicted overshoot) commands more brake.
    The law is gated to the unpowered-ascent window, returning $0$ whenever $T(t)>0$ or $v_z\geq0$;
    that window belongs to the law and is not the trigger below.
    \item \textbf{Full}: $f_b=1$ from the trigger instant onward and $0$ before: a constant area,
    opened once. A sizing study solves against this variant: with $f_b$ a step, a target apogee
    fixes either $C_{D,b}S_b$ or the deployment time, one unknown against one equation.
\end{itemize}
Every law is \emph{memoryless}, re-evaluated from the current state at every right-hand-side call,
since the solver evaluates at arbitrary, repeated and rejected times. A law with memory needs the
sampled mode below; none is written.

\subsubsection{Deployment trigger}\label{sec:airbrake_trigger}
Which condition arms the brake, chosen independently of the law. Each variant is an \emph{onset}
latch: once true it stays armed.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{After burnout} (default): $T(t)\leq0$, true from burnout onward.
    \item \textbf{After time}: $t\geq t_{brake}$, with $t_{brake}=0$ (default) meaning deployed from
    $t=0$.
    \item \textbf{After apogee}: $v_z>0$, the canopy's apogee predicate, with the same caveat: a
    pre-apogee transient in $v_z$ would arm it early.
\end{itemize}
Only the \textbf{full} law reads the trigger; \textbf{none} ignores it and \textbf{proportional}
uses its own window. No condition retracts an armed brake, and there is no deployment-rate limit
between $f_b=0$ and $1$; a real actuator has both, and both are future work.

\subsection{Sampled-data evaluation (zero-order hold)}\label{sec:sampled}
This is an interchangeable \emph{mode of evaluating} the models, changing \emph{when} a quantity is
computed, not \emph{what} it is. By default every model is a pure function of $(t,\vec{y})$
(\autoref{eq:state_vector}), re-evaluated at every right-hand-side call. A flight computer's
measure--compute--hold cycle, an integral term or a filter with internal state cannot be expressed
that way; this mode makes it expressible.

With a sample rate $f_s>0$ and period $T_s=1/f_s$, the ticks are $t_k=kT_s$. At each tick every
registered \emph{channel} --- a named sampled quantity --- is evaluated once, at that tick's state,
and held:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:zoh}
    c(t) = c_k \equiv c\!\left(t_k,\vec{y}(t_k);\,c_{k-1}\right), \qquad t\in[t_k,t_{k+1}).
\end{equation}
A channel receives \emph{its own} previous held value $c_{k-1}$ (undefined at the first tick). That
makes it recursive, and so an integral accumulator, a rate limiter's previous command or a discrete
filter state are each a function of this form. One channel exists, the deployment fraction $f_b$;
its laws are memoryless and ignore $c_{k-1}$, so the mode currently changes only \emph{how often}
$f_b$ is computed.

Three requirements follow from \autoref{eq:zoh}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Fixed-step integration, with $T_s$ an exact integer multiple of the step.} An
    adaptive step cannot honour a hold boundary, and $T_s/h$ must be an integer so that every
    boundary falls on a step boundary. A non-integer ratio is rejected rather than rounded, which
    would silently change the sample rate.
    \item \textbf{The hold is constant across every stage of a substep.} The right-hand side is then
    smooth inside a tick and the integrator's order is preserved; every discontinuity lands on a
    step boundary.
    \item \textbf{The sample is taken at the tick's start}, $(t_k,\vec{y}(t_k))$: measure, compute,
    apply until the next tick, the causal order of a flight computer.
\end{enumerate}
Two properties of a sampled run are not defects. It is a \emph{different dynamical system} from the
continuous one, not a more accurate solution of it; the meaningful comparison is ``this actuator at
this sample rate'' against ``an ideal continuous actuator''. And it is substantially more expensive
(small fixed steps for the whole flight), which is why it is opt-in: $f_s=0$ is the default, and
the mode is then structurally absent. It supplies the place to run a controller with memory, and
nothing else: no such controller and no state-coupled turbulence model is written here.
