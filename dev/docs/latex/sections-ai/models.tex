\section{Models}\label{sec:models}
\begin{aitext}
Each subsection gives the interchangeable models behind one term of the equations of motion: the
three forces (gravity, thrust with its mass, aerodynamics with the relative flow), the environment
they depend on (atmosphere, wind), the random disturbances, the Earth-rotation correction, and the
integration. The last subsection, sampled-data evaluation, changes when the models are evaluated
and is off by default. The launch rail, recovery and air brake, which modify the equations, are in
\autoref{sec:other_dynamics}.
\end{aitext}

\subsection{Gravity}\label{sec:gravity}
This subsection supplies the weight $\vec{W}=mg\hat{z}_g$: the magnitude $g$ and the direction
$\hat{z}_g$ (down, along the local vertical), chosen independently. Below, $h$ is the altitude above
the surface and $R_e$ the Earth's mean radius; \autoref{sec:position_altitude} defines the altitudes.

\subsubsection{Magnitude}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Constant:} $g=g_0$.
    \item \textbf{Radial} (inverse-square, spherical non-rotating Earth).
    \item \textbf{Ellipsoidal} (WGS84, experimental): Somigliana surface gravity plus a
    second-order free-air correction, with $\phi$ the geodetic latitude of the launch site and
    constants from \autoref{sec:constants}.
\end{itemize}
\begin{gather}
    g(r)=g_0\left(\frac{R_e}{r}\right)^2 \qquad \text{(radial)} \\
    \gamma_0(\phi) = \gamma_e\frac{1+k\sin^2\phi}{\sqrt{1-e^2\sin^2\phi}} \\
    g(\phi, h) = \gamma_0\left[1 - \frac{2}{a_e}\left(1+f+m-2f\sin^2\phi\right)h + \frac{3}{a_e^2}h^2\right].
\end{gather}
The ellipsoidal magnitude already contains the centrifugal part of the Earth's rotation.

\paragraph{Radius in the radial model.} Here $r=|\vec{Og}|$ is the distance from the Earth's
centre $O$ to the rocket's centre of gravity $g$, and $R_e$ is a constant. At launch
\begin{equation}
    r(0)=R_0=R_e+h_0+h_{g,0},
\end{equation}
with $h_0$ the launch-site altitude above the mean Earth radius and $h_{g,0}$ the height of the
initial centre of gravity above the ground (on the pad or rail), so that the initial position of
$g$ relative to $O$ is consistent with the site. Later, $r=R_0+(\text{height gained})$, to first
order in the curvature. The local radius of the real Earth is not constant: it varies with
latitude, $R(\phi)\approx a_e(1-f\sin^2\phi)$, by $0.3\%$ between equator and pole, which is
$0.7\%$ in $g$; the ellipsoidal model above accounts for it. Ground-height models (terrain or
geoid undulation) could replace $h_0$ and the flat ground by a position-dependent surface, as an
option.

\subsubsection{Direction}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Parallel} (default): $\hat{z}_g\equiv\hat{z}_0$, the local vertical at the launch
    point, for the whole flight.
    \item \textbf{Radial:} $\hat{z}_g$ points to the centre $O$ of a sphere of radius $R_e$.
    \item \textbf{Ellipsoidal:} $\hat{z}_g$ is the inward normal of the WGS84 ellipsoid.
\end{itemize}
With $\vec{Og}=\vec{OO^*}+\vec{O^*g}$, $\vec{OO^*}=-R_e\hat{z}_0$ and $\vec{O^*g}=x\hat{x}_0+z\hat{z}_0$
in the pitch plane,
\begin{align}
    \text{parallel:}\quad & \hat{z}_g = \hat{z}_0, \\
    \text{radial:}\quad & \hat{z}_g = -\frac{\vec{Og}}{|\vec{Og}|}
        = -\sin\varphi\,\hat{x}_0 + \cos\varphi\,\hat{z}_0, \qquad \tan\varphi=\frac{x}{R_e-z}.
        \label{eq:zhat_g_rotated}
\end{align}
\simtodo{uses the arc angle $\varphi=x/R_e$ (\autoref{eq:phi_downrange}), which agrees with the
exact angle at $z=0$ and to first order elsewhere}

For the ellipsoid the normal does not pass through $O$: it makes the angle $\phi-\psi$ with $\vec{Og}$,
where $\psi$ is the geocentric latitude, $\tan\psi=(1-e^2)\tan\phi$ (at most $0.19^\circ$). The
normal at the rocket is the normal at $O^*$ turned by the change of geodetic latitude along the
track, $\vartheta=\int_0^x \mathrm{d}x'/R_n(x')$, with $R_n$ the local normal-curvature radius, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zhat_g_ellipsoid}
    \hat{z}_g = -\sin\vartheta\,\hat{x}_0 + \cos\vartheta\,\hat{z}_0 .
\end{equation}
Freezing $R_n$ at its launch value gives the radius of the osculating sphere in the flight
azimuth $\chi$ (Euler's formula, with $M,N$ the meridional and prime-vertical radii of curvature),
\begin{equation}
    R_\chi = \left(\frac{\cos^2\chi}{M(\phi_0)} + \frac{\sin^2\chi}{N(\phi_0)}\right)^{-1},
    \quad M = \frac{a_e(1-e^2)}{(1-e^2\sin^2\phi)^{3/2}}, \quad
    N = \frac{a_e}{\sqrt{1-e^2\sin^2\phi}},
\end{equation}
so $\vartheta=x/R_\chi$, i.e.\ \autoref{eq:zhat_g_rotated} with $R_e\to R_\chi$.
This differs from the exact normal at third order in $\vartheta$, because $R_n$ keeps changing
with latitude along the track.

\subsubsection{Vector field (magnitude + direction)}
A single model may supply $g$ and $\hat{z}_g$ together, as a vector field
$\vec{g}(\vec{x},t)$, so that $\vec{W}=m\vec{g}$. The radial model above is already the point-mass
field; the refinements are
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{J2:} the second zonal harmonic (oblateness), $J_2=1.0826\times10^{-3}$. With the
    Earth's rotation it changes $g$ by $\approx0.53\%$ from equator to pole; the altitude effect is
    $\approx-0.3\%$ at $10\,\mathrm{km}$ (free-air gradient $-3.086\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{s^{-2}}$).
    \item \textbf{Spherical-harmonic geopotential} (EGM2008 \cite{pavlis}), to a chosen degree and
    order, with local anomalies folded in: what a truncated global model misses.
\end{itemize}
The ellipsoidal magnitude above already contains the latitude variation; the remaining terms are
negligible for a rocket flying below $10\,\mathrm{km}$.

\subsubsection{Position relative to $O$ and true altitude}\label{sec:position_altitude}
The non-parallel models need the altitude $h_{true}$ over the curved ground, which the
altitude-dependent models use in place of $-z$. The arc angle and the ground point under the
rocket are
\begin{gather}
    \varphi = \frac{x}{R_e}, \label{eq:phi_downrange}\\
    \vec{O^*P}(\varphi) = R_e\sin\varphi\,\hat{x}_0 + R_e(1-\cos\varphi)\,\hat{z}_0,
        \label{eq:surface_point}
\end{gather}
and the altitude is the offset of the rocket from that point along $-\hat{z}_g$,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:h_true}
    h_{true}(x,z) = \left[x\hat{x}_0+z\hat{z}_0-\vec{O^*P}(\varphi)\right]\cdot\bigl(-\hat{z}_g\bigr)
        = x\sin\varphi - z\cos\varphi - R_e(1-\cos\varphi).
\end{equation}
For small $\varphi$, $h_{true}\approx x^2/2R_e-z$. Impact is $h_{true}=0$ instead of $z=0$. For the
ellipsoid, $R_e\to R_\chi$. Replacing $R_e$ by $R_0$ would change the angles by a relative
$h_0/R_e$, below $10^{-3}$ for a site under $6\,\mathrm{km}$, so $R_e$ is kept.
\figtodo{Diagram (TikZ, exaggerated curvature): the Earth's centre $O$, launch site $O^*$, rocket
position $g$, arc angle $\varphi$, the rotated $\hat{z}_g$ and the altitude $h_{true}$ over the
curved ground.}

Which position matters depends on the question asked of the flight:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Relative to the launch point} (downrange $x$, height above the site): apogee,
    recovery and reporting.
    \item \textbf{Above mean sea level or the geoid:} atmosphere and regulations.
    \item \textbf{From the Earth's centre, $r=R_e+h_0+\dots$:} gravity.
    \item \textbf{Height above the local curved ground, $h_{true}$:} impact and terrain clearance.
\end{itemize}
The simulator integrates $(x,z)$ relative to the launch point and reports the height above the
ground $h_{true}$ (equal to $-z$ for parallel gravity). The atmosphere uses $h_{true}+h_0$, the
wind shear $h_{true}$.

\subsection{Thrust}\label{sec:thrust}
\subsubsection{Magnitude}
Thrust comes from the momentum of the ejected propellant and the pressure difference at the
nozzle exit,
\begin{equation}
    T = \left|\dot m_m\right| v_e + A_e(p_e - p_{atm}),
\end{equation}
with $\dot m_m$ the motor mass flow, $v_e$ the exhaust speed, $p_e$ the exit pressure, $A_e$ the exit
area and $p_{atm}$ the ambient pressure. In practice a static-test curve is used instead. The
\emph{reference} thrust $T_0(t)$ is given as points $(t_i,T_i)$, $i=1,\dots,N$, from a motor file,
interpolated linearly, held at $T_1$ for $t<t_1$ and zero for $t>t_N$; burnout is its falling
zero-crossing:
\begin{equation}
    T_0(t) = \mathrm{lerp}\!\left(t; \{t_i\}, \{T_i\}\right).
\end{equation}
The test was run at a fixed ambient pressure $p_0$. While the motor is flowing ($T_0>0$), the
thrust in flight is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thrust_pressure}
    T(t, h) = T_0(t) + A_e\left(p_0 - p_{atm}(h)\right),
\end{equation}
which rises with altitude by at most $A_ep_0$ in vacuum; $A_e=0$ recovers $T_0(t)$ exactly.

\subsubsection{Direction}
The line of thrust may differ from the nose axis by an angle $\theta_t$. Its unit vector
$\hat{x}_t$ follows from $\hat{x}_r$ exactly as $\hat{x}_r$ does from $\hat{x}_0$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thrust_frame}
    \hat{x}_t = \cos\theta_t\,\hat{x}_r - \sin\theta_t\,\hat{z}_r, \qquad
    \hat{z}_t = \sin\theta_t\,\hat{x}_r + \cos\theta_t\,\hat{z}_r, \qquad \hat{y}_t\equiv\hat{y}_r,
\end{equation}
so a positive $\theta_t$ tilts the thrust line the way a positive $\theta_r$ tilts the nose. Three
options:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ideal} (default): $\theta_t=0$.
    \item \textbf{Misalignment}: $\theta_t$ is a constant input, a manufacturing-type error.
    \item \textbf{Controlled orientation} (gimbal): $\theta_t(t)$ commanded by a control law, the
    only pitch authority once off the rail. Future work.
\end{itemize}
The axial component is $T\cos\theta_t$ ($4\times10^{-5}$ lost at $0.5^\circ$), while
$T\sin\theta_t$ acts at the arm $l_t-l_g$ and matters as a moment.

\subsection{Mass, centre of gravity and moment of inertia}\label{sec:mass}
As propellant burns, mass leaves through the nozzle and both the centre of gravity and the moment
of inertia change. The rocket is split into two bodies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the \emph{dry rocket} (subscript $r$), everything that does not burn: constant mass
    $m_r$, centre of gravity $l_{g,r}$ from the nose tip $A$, inertia $I_{r,g}$ about its own
    centre of gravity;
    \item the \emph{motor} (subscript $m$), casing and remaining propellant: mass $m_m(t)$, centre
    of gravity $l_{g,m}(t)$, inertia $I_{m,g}(t)$ about its own centre of gravity.
\end{itemize}
The subscript $g$ in $I_{r,g}$, $I_{m,g}$ means ``about that body's own centre of gravity''; the
totals $m$, $l_g$, $I$ carry no body subscript. $t$ is time since ignition. The subscript $p$ is not
used for the propellant, as it names the aerodynamic reference point. $g_0$ is the standard
gravity of the definition of specific impulse, unrelated to the local $g(h)$.

\subsubsection{Mass}\label{sec:mass_mass}
The total mass is the dry rocket plus the motor,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_total}
    m(t) = m_r + m_m(t).
\end{equation}
Only $m_m$ changes. It starts at $m_0$ and follows the mass flow until burnout, after which it is
held at $m_f$ (the casing and whatever else stays; $m_f=0$ if $m_m$ counts propellant only):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_motor}
    m_m(t) = m_0 + \int_0^t \dot m_m\,\mathrm{d}t', \qquad m_m\ge m_f.
\end{equation}
The mass flow $\dot m_m<0$ follows the thrust, as in the specific-impulse model below.
\simtodo{the state $m$ is integrated without a floor at $m_f$; only the propellant part is clamped
at zero for the centre of gravity and inertia, so an $\mathrm{ISP}$ inconsistent with the thrust

\paragraph{Specific-impulse model.} Inputs: the thrust curve and the specific impulse
$\mathrm{ISP}$ (seconds). The propellant leaves at the rate that delivers exactly that thrust,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_isp}
    \dot m_m = -\frac{T_0(t)}{\mathrm{ISP}\;g_0},
\end{equation}
integrated until $m_m$ reaches $m_f$. It uses the \emph{reference} curve $T_0(t)$, not the
pressure-corrected $T$, because $\mathrm{ISP}$ is defined from the stand measurement; the exit
pressure term changes the force on the airframe, not the propellant consumption: the mass flow of
a choked nozzle is set by the chamber conditions and the throat area, not by the ambient pressure
\cite{sutton}. If $\mathrm{ISP}$
is not given, the effective value $\mathrm{ISP}=J/(\Delta m\,g_0)$, with $J=\int T_0\,\mathrm{d}t$
and $\Delta m=m_0-m_f$, makes the integrated mass flow remove exactly $\Delta m$; the mass then
decreases in proportion to the delivered impulse, which is the model used by OpenRocket and
RocketPy \cite{ordev,hpr}.

\subsubsection{Centre of gravity}\label{sec:mass_cog}
The first moment of mass about the nose tip $A$ is additive:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_cog}
    l_g(t)\,m(t) = l_{g,r}\,m_r + l_{g,m}(t)\,m_m(t)
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    l_g(t) = \frac{l_{g,r}\,m_r + l_{g,m}(t)\,m_m(t)}{m(t)}.
\end{equation}
The same holds for a non-zero radial offset,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_cog_r}
    r_g(t)\,m(t) = r_{g,r}\,m_r + r_{g,m}(t)\,m_m(t).
\end{equation}
The simplest model of the motor's own centre of gravity is linear in the \emph{burned} mass
$m_b=m_0-m_m$, with $l_0$, $l_f$ its values at ignition and burnout and $\Delta m=m_0-m_f$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_l_linear}
    \frac{\partial l_{g,m}}{\partial m_b}=\frac{l_f-l_0}{\Delta m},\qquad
    l_{g,m}(m_m)=l_0+\frac{l_f-l_0}{\Delta m}\,(m_0-m_m).
\end{equation}
(With respect to $m_m$ itself the sign of the derivative would flip.)

\subsubsection{Moment of inertia}\label{sec:mass_inertia}
The inertias add once each body's own inertia is transferred to the total centre of gravity $g$
by the parallel-axis theorem,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_inertia}
    I(t) = I_r + I_m(t),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_inertia_parts}
    I_r = I_{r,g} + m_r\,d_r^2, \qquad I_m(t) = I_{m,g}(t) + m_m(t)\,d_m(t)^2,
\end{equation}
with the transfer distances in the pitch plane
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_dist}
    d_r^2=\bigl(l_g(t)-l_{g,r}\bigr)^2+\bigl(r_g(t)-r_{g,r}\bigr)^2, \qquad
    d_m^2=\bigl(l_g(t)-l_{g,m}(t)\bigr)^2+\bigl(r_g(t)-r_{g,m}(t)\bigr)^2.
\end{equation}
On the axis ($r\equiv0$) only the axial terms remain. $I_r$ depends on time through $l_g(t)$
although $I_{r,g}$ and $m_r$ do not.

The motor's own inertia is modelled like its centre of gravity, linear in burned mass, with $I_0$,
$I_f$ its values at ignition and burnout:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_I_linear}
    \frac{\partial I_{m,g}}{\partial m_b}=\frac{I_f-I_0}{\Delta m},\qquad
    I_{m,g}(m_m)=I_0+\frac{I_f-I_0}{\Delta m}\,(m_0-m_m).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Dry-rocket inputs from components.} As in OpenRocket \cite{orguide}, the dry-rocket
inputs can be built from the components $i$, each with mass $m_i$ (measured, or density times
volume), centre of gravity $l_i$ and own inertia $I_i$:
\begin{equation}
    m_r=\sum_i m_i, \qquad l_{g,r}=\frac{\sum_i m_il_i}{m_r}, \qquad
    I_{r,g}=\sum_i\left(I_i+m_i(l_i-l_{g,r})^2\right).
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Propellant grain geometry}\label{sec:mass_grain}
A solid motor's $m_m(t)$, $l_{g,m}(t)$ and $I_{m,g}(t)$ follow from the receding grain. The
propellant burns normal to its surface at the rate $r=a_b\,p_c^{\,n}$ ($n<1$ for stable burning),
and the burning area $A_b$ over the throat area $A_t$ is $K_n=A_b/A_t$. Equating the mass generated,
$\rho_{pr}A_b\,a_bp_c^{\,n}$, to the mass discharged, $p_cA_t/c^*$, gives
\begin{equation} \label{eq:grain_pressure}
    p_c = \left(a_b\,\rho_{pr}\,c^*K_n\right)^{1/(1-n)}, \qquad
    \dot m_m = -\rho_{pr}A_b\,r,
\end{equation}
with $\rho_{pr}$ the propellant density and $c^*$ the characteristic velocity \cite{nakka}. The web
regression $\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t=r$ is integrated until the web is consumed. For $N$ BATES
segments (outer surface inhibited, bore diameter $d=d_0+2x$, length $L=L_0-2x$, outer diameter $D$):
\begin{equation} \label{eq:grain_bates}
    A_b(x) = N\left[\tfrac{\pi}{2}\left(D^2-d^2\right) + \pi\,d\,L\right],
\end{equation}
with the maximum at $x=(L_0-2d_0)/6$. A segment has mass $m=\rho_{pr}\tfrac{\pi}{4}(D^2-d^2)L$ and
inertias $I_{ax}=\tfrac18 m(D^2+d^2)$, $I_{tr}=\tfrac{m}{12}\bigl[\tfrac34(D^2+d^2)+L^2\bigr]$ about
its own centre. Both ends regress equally, so each segment's centre of gravity stays fixed; the
stack follows from the segment masses and the parallel-axis theorem. The simulator would need
$a_b$, $n$, $\rho_{pr}$, $c^*$, the segment geometry and the throat and exit areas \cite{openmotor}.

\begin{aitext}
\subsection{Aerodynamics}\label{sec:aero}
\subsubsection{Relative velocity and angle of attack}\label{sec:relwind}
With $\vec{v}_w$ the wind velocity in $\mathcal{F}_0$ (\autoref{sec:wind}), the rocket's velocity
relative to the air is
\begin{equation}
    \vec{v}_{rel} = \vec{v}_r - \vec{v}_w = v_{rel,x}\hat{x}_r + v_{rel,z}\hat{z}_r,
\end{equation}
with $v_{rel,x}$ the axial and $v_{rel,z}$ the normal component in $\mathcal{F}_r$. The airspeed and
the angle of attack, taken in the body frame in the range $(-180^\circ,180^\circ]$, are
\begin{equation}
    v_{rel} = \sqrt{v_{rel,x}^2+v_{rel,z}^2}, \qquad \alpha = \operatorname{atan2}(v_{rel,z}, v_{rel,x}).
\end{equation}
A rocket flying nose-first has $\alpha\approx0$, ascending or descending; a tail-first one has
$\alpha\approx180^\circ$. They feed $q$ and, through $\alpha$, the coefficient lookup.

\subsubsection{Force and moment}
The force $\vec{A}_f$ and moment $\vec{A}_m$ of \autoref{eq:aero_magnitudes} are \emph{definitions},
not a model: they hold for every coefficient model, and the force is never supplied directly. $S$
is the cross-section of the body tube and $c$ its diameter.
One pair $(S,c)$ per component is shared by all of its coefficient models, since a coefficient
means nothing except relative to the pair it was normalised by.

\paragraph{Multi-component aerodynamics.} When the airframe is built from several components $k$
(body, fins, nose cone, \dots), each with its own reference point $pp_k$, force $\vec{A}_{f,k}$ and
moment $\vec{A}_{m,k}$ about $pp_k$, the total force is the sum and the total moment about $p$
adds the moment of each force about $p$:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:aero_multi}
    \vec{A}_f = \sum_k \vec{A}_{f,k}, \qquad
    \vec{A}_m(p) = \sum_k \vec{A}_{m,k} + \sum_k \vec{p\,pp_k}\times\vec{A}_{f,k},
\end{equation}
where $\vec{p\,pp_k}=-\vec{Ap}+\vec{A\,pp_k}$ is the position of $pp_k$ relative to $p$, the arm of
the force acting at $pp_k$. Each component is normalised by its
own reference area and length, $\vec{A}_{f,k}=-qS_k(C_{x,k}\hat{x}_r+C_{z,k}\hat{z}_r)$ and
$\vec{A}_{m,k}=-qS_kc_k\,C_{m,k}\hat{y}_r$, so $S_k,c_k$ generally differ from the airframe's $S,c$.
\simtodo{only a single aerodynamic component (one $S,c$ and one coefficient set per airframe) is
implemented; \autoref{eq:aero_multi} is not}

$l_p-l_g$ is the axial distance from $g$ to $p$, positive with $p$ aft, so that
$\vec{gp}=-(l_p-l_g)\hat{x}_r$ and the caliber static margin is $\mathrm{SM}=(l_p-l_g)/c$.

\paragraph{Axial force and the flow direction.} The axial force must oppose the axial component of the relative velocity, $v_{rel,x}=\hat{x}_r\cdot
\vec{v}_{rel}$. In the body-axis form $-qSC_x\hat{x}_r$ with $C_x>0$ it always points aft, so it
would push a tail-first rocket faster. The coefficient is therefore multiplied by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:axial_factor}
    f(\alpha)=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\cos\alpha}{\cos 85^\circ},\,-1,\,1\right),
\end{equation}
a smooth sign of the axial component: $\approx+1$ nose-first, $\approx-1$ tail-first, and linear
through zero between $85^\circ$ and $95^\circ$ so that the force does not jump. In
\autoref{eq:aero_magnitudes} $C_x$ stands for $f\,C_x$. A table that itself covers
$|\alpha|>90^\circ$ is used as it is.
\simtodo{$f(\alpha)$ fixes only the sign of the axial force, with a linear ramp near $90^\circ$;
the magnitude law of \autoref{eq:cx_full} is not implemented}

\paragraph{Large angles of attack.} CFD and wind-tunnel tables will not cover the full $\alpha$
range, and the drag \emph{magnitude}, not only its sign, changes with $\alpha$. Jorgensen's
semi-empirical model for bodies of revolution \cite{jorgensen} scales the axial coefficient and adds
a viscous crossflow term to the normal force,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:cx_full}
    C_x(\alpha) = \begin{cases}
        C_x(0)\cos^2\alpha, & 0\le\alpha\le90^\circ, \\
        C_x(180^\circ)\cos^2(180^\circ-\alpha), & 90^\circ<\alpha\le180^\circ,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:cz_crossflow}
    C_z = \frac{S_{base}}{S}\sin2\alpha\,\cos\frac{\alpha}{2}
        + \eta\,C_{d,n}\,\frac{S_p}{S}\sin^2\alpha,
\end{equation}
with $S_{base}$ the base area, $S_p$ the planform area, $\eta$ the finite-length factor (ratio of
the drag of a finite cylinder to that of an infinite one) and $C_{d,n}$ the crossflow drag
coefficient, evaluated at $M\sin\alpha$ and $Re\sin\alpha$ ($1.2$ at low crossflow Mach number).
The first term of \autoref{eq:cz_crossflow} is slender-body potential flow, the second is the
viscous crossflow. The method is verified mainly up to about $20^\circ$, and over the full range
only at a few Mach numbers \cite{jorgensen}. In practice the table is used at small $\alpha$ and a
semi-empirical model beyond the table's range, as in OpenRocket and RASAero
\cite{ortech,rasaerom}.

\subsubsection{Dynamic pressure}
\begin{equation}
    q = \tfrac12\rho v_{rel}^2,
\end{equation}
with $\rho$ the local air density and $v_{rel}$ the airspeed above.

\subsubsection{Coefficients}\label{sec:aero_coeffs}
The coefficients $C_x,C_z,C_m$ and the centre of pressure $l_p$ are not derived from the airframe's
shape; they are supplied. They depend on the angle of attack $\alpha$, the Mach number $M$ and the
Reynolds number $Re$ (\autoref{sec:reynolds_mach}).
The pitch rate enters through the non-dimensional $\hat{\omega}=\omega_rc/(2v_{rel})$. The rate of
change of the angle of attack is in practice lumped with it, since forced-oscillation tests and CFD
give only the sum of the two derivatives \cite{cobalt}. Power-on or power-off (base drag) and surface
roughness enter as discrete or parametric inputs, i.e.\ extra table axes or offsets
\cite{rasaerom}; accelerations are not used as inputs in standard practice
\cite{datcom,rasaerom}.
Three interchangeable models supply them, selected per run, in order of increasing fidelity; $C$
stands for any of $C_x,C_z,C_m$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Constant}: independent of the flight state, with $l_p$ fixed. Useful for
    first-cut sizing and for isolating one effect in a sweep.
    \begin{equation}
        C = C_0
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Linearized}, about a reference condition in $\alpha$, $M$, $Re$ and the
    non-dimensional pitch rate, with $l_p$ fixed; valid for small $\alpha$. The derivatives with
    respect to $\hat{\omega}$ are $C_{m,q}$ and $C_{z,q}$.
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:aero_general_linear}
        C(\alpha,M,Re,\hat{\omega}) = C_0 + \alpha\frac{\partial C}{\partial\alpha}
            + M\frac{\partial C}{\partial M} + Re\frac{\partial C}{\partial Re}
            + \hat{\omega}\frac{\partial C}{\partial\hat{\omega}}
    \end{equation}
    If $C_x$ must grow with $\alpha$, the consistent body-axis option is a quadratic term,
    $C_x=C_{x,0}+C_{x,\alpha^2}\,\alpha^2$; the drag polar $C_D(C_L)$ is a wind-axis form for
    lifting bodies and would count again the drag already contained in $C_x(\alpha)$.
    \item \textbf{Lookup table}: tabulated over $(\alpha,M)$ from CFD or wind-tunnel data and
    interpolated at run time. The table gives the centre of pressure $l_p$ from the nose, not the
    arm: CFD and wind-tunnel data know nothing of the centre of gravity, and the arm $l_p-l_g$ is
    formed from the live $l_g(t)$. This is the only model in which the static margin responds to
    the flight state.
    \begin{equation}
        \bigl(C_x,\,C_z,\,C_m,\,l_p\bigr) = \mathrm{interp}\bigl(\alpha,M;\ \text{table}\bigr)
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Atmosphere}\label{sec:atmosphere}
Provides the air density $\rho$, speed of sound $a$ and dynamic viscosity $\mu$ at altitude $h$
(and horizontal position), which form the Reynolds and Mach numbers. Turbulence and gusts are in
\autoref{sec:noise}.

\subsubsection{Model tiers}\label{sec:atmos_tiers}
Three tiers, one interface $(\rho, T, p, a, \mu)$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{ISA}: standard layered atmosphere (\autoref{tab:isa_layers}).
    \item \textbf{Sounding}: one measured $(T, p)$ profile, interpolated in altitude.
    \item \textbf{Field}: several stations, interpolated in altitude and horizontal position.
    \simtodo{interpolation in time between soundings is not implemented}
\end{enumerate}
\comptodo{ISA, one sounding (a demo station from the data folder) and the multi-station field on
the demo rocket; the table gives apogee, downrange and maximum dynamic pressure, the plot $T$, $p$,
$\rho$ against altitude for ISA and the sounding, the difference shaded. Generated by the
simulator.}

\paragraph{ISA.} Geopotential altitude from the geometric altitude above mean sea level $h_{msl}$:
\begin{equation}
    H = \frac{R_{isa}h_{msl}}{R_{isa}+h_{msl}}, \qquad R_{isa} = 6356766\,\mathrm{m}.
\end{equation}
Within the layer of \autoref{tab:isa_layers} containing $H$, temperature is linear in geopotential altitude:
\begin{equation}
    T = T_b + L(H-H_b).
\end{equation}
For a non-zero lapse rate the pressure follows the polytropic relation,
\begin{equation}
    p = p_b(T/T_b)^{-g_{isa}/(LR_d)},
\end{equation}
and for an isothermal layer ($L=0$) the exponential form,
\begin{equation}
    p = p_b\exp\!\left(\frac{-g_{isa}(H-H_b)}{R_dT_b}\right).
\end{equation}
Density, speed of sound and viscosity follow from the ideal gas law and Sutherland's law:
\begin{equation}
    \rho = \frac{p}{R_dT}, \qquad a = \sqrt{\gamma R_dT}, \qquad
    \mu = \mu_0\left(\frac{T}{T_0}\right)^{3/2}\frac{T_0+S_\mu}{T+S_\mu},
\end{equation}
with $R_d = 287.0528\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$, $\gamma = 1.4$, $g_{isa} = 9.80665\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ (the
ISA's own fixed value), $\mu_0 = 1.716\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$,
$T_0 = 273.15\,\mathrm{K}$, $S_\mu = 110.4\,\mathrm{K}$.

\paragraph{Sounding.} One station's measured $(h_{msl}, p, T, u, v)$ profile, with temperature and
wind interpolated linearly and pressure log-linearly:
\begin{equation}
    T(h) = \mathrm{lerp}(h;\{h_i\},\{T_i\}), \quad
    p(h) = \exp\mathrm{lerp}(h;\{h_i\},\{\ln p_i\}), \quad
    u(h),v(h) = \mathrm{lerp}(h;\{h_i\},\{u_i,v_i\}).
\end{equation}
$\rho, a, \mu$ follow from the interpolated $T, p$ as for ISA. Below the lowest level the profile
continues with the sounding's own lapse rate; above the highest, an ISA-shaped profile re-anchored
at the top level keeps $T, p$ continuous; wind is held at the edge value.

\paragraph{Field.} $N$ stations are projected onto a tangent plane centred at the launch site
$(\phi_0,\lambda_0)$, together with the rocket's ground position $(x\sin\chi, x\cos\chi)$:
\begin{equation}
    E = R_e\cos\phi_0\,(\lambda-\lambda_0), \qquad N = R_e\,(\phi-\phi_0).
\end{equation}
The $N$ profiles are combined, weighted by inverse squared distance $d_i$, into one profile:
\begin{equation}
    w_i = \frac{1}{d_i^2+\varepsilon^2}, \qquad \hat{w}_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_j w_j}, \qquad
    (T, \ln p, u, v) = \sum_i \hat{w}_i\,(T_i, \ln p_i, u_i, v_i),
\end{equation}
then interpolated in altitude as in the sounding tier. A single station reduces every
$\hat{w}_i$ to 1 and reproduces the sounding tier.
\simtodo{each station is first resampled onto a common altitude grid ($0$ to $30\,\mathrm{km}$ in
$100\,\mathrm{m}$ steps) before weighting, so a single station reproduces the sounding tier only to
the error of that grid interpolation}

Inverse-distance weighting is defined everywhere, also outside the polygon the stations span, and
never oscillates; $\varepsilon$ (a few hundred metres) removes the singularity at zero distance. A
thin-plate-spline radial basis function is available as an alternative.

\subsubsection{Density}\label{sec:density}
Water vapour is lighter than dry air ($M_v=18.02$ against $M_d=28.96\,\mathrm{g/mol}$), so at a
given pressure and temperature damp air is \emph{less} dense. The effect is at the percent level,
and radiosondes report the dewpoint needed to correct for it.

\paragraph{Partial pressures.} Treat the mixture as two ideal gases sharing a volume, dry air at
partial pressure $p-e$ and vapour at $e$:
\begin{equation}
    \rho = \frac{p-e}{R_dT} + \frac{e}{R_vT}
         = \frac{p}{R_dT}\left[1 - \frac{e}{p}\left(1 - \epsilon\right)\right], \qquad
    \epsilon = \frac{R_d}{R_v} = \frac{287.0528}{461.5} = 0.6220.
\end{equation}
The bracket is always below one. Folding it into an effective \emph{virtual temperature}, the
temperature dry air would need to have the moist density at the same pressure, keeps the
dry-air gas constant $R_d$ in every formula:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:virtual_temperature}
    T_v = \frac{T}{1 - \dfrac{e}{p}\left(1-\epsilon\right)}, \qquad
    \rho = \frac{p}{R_dT_v},
\end{equation}
with $T_v \geq T$, equality for dry air.

\paragraph{Vapour pressure from dewpoint.} The dewpoint $T_d$ is the temperature to which the
parcel must be cooled, at constant pressure and mixing ratio, to reach saturation, so the vapour
pressure is the saturation pressure at the dewpoint, $e=e_s(T_d)$. For $e_s$ the Magnus form is
used, with the Alduchov--Eskridge coefficients, $T_d$ in $^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $e_s$ in hPa:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:magnus}
    e_s(T_d) = 6.1094\,\exp\!\left(\frac{17.625\,T_d}{T_d + 243.04}\right).
\end{equation}
It is accurate to better than $0.4\%$ over $-40$ to $+50\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. The factor of 100
converting hPa to Pa before forming $e/p$ is the easy mistake.

The correction is an opt-in switch, off by default (dry air, $\rho=p/(R_dT)$), and applies to the
sounding and field tiers. At a warm, humid surface ($T=30\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$,
$T_d=26\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$) it changes the density by $-1.25\%$, at a temperate one
($T=14.2\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $T_d=10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$) by $-0.48\%$, near the tropopause by less
than $10^{-5}$. The speed of sound and viscosity are not corrected ($\sim0.2\%$).

\subsubsection{Reynolds and Mach numbers}\label{sec:reynolds_mach}
\begin{equation}
    Re = \frac{\rho v_{rel} l_{Re}}{\mu}, \qquad M = \frac{v_{rel}}{a},
\end{equation}
with $v_{rel}$ the airspeed, $l_{Re}$ the Reynolds reference length, a separate input
rather than the body diameter $c$, $\mu$ the dynamic viscosity and $a$ the
local speed of sound. No coefficient model uses $Re$ yet.

\end{aitext}

\begin{aitext}
\subsection{Wind}\label{sec:wind}
Goal: given time $t$ and position (altitude $h$, and horizontal position for the interpolated
tier), provide the wind velocity $\vec{v}_w$ in $\mathcal{F}_0$. The aerodynamic force and moment
see the air only through $\vec{v}_{rel} = \vec{v}_r - \vec{v}_w$. The tiers mirror the atmosphere:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Simple analytic model}: constant wind plus an altitude-dependent shear profile,
    with an optional turbulence term (\autoref{sec:noise}).
    \item \textbf{Real data} (one sounding): a measured or forecast profile $\vec{v}_w(h)$ at one
    location and time, or an average for that location.
    \item \textbf{Real data across multiple stations}: profiles from several locations,
    interpolated in altitude and horizontal position by inverse-distance weighting, as for the
    atmosphere (\texttt{sim/atmosphere.py}).
    \simtodo{interpolation in time between soundings is not implemented}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Flight-plane projection.} The launch heading $\chi$ fixes the 2D flight plane. A
horizontal wind given as ENU components $(u,v)$, or as meteorological speed and direction
$(W,\psi)$ with $u=-W\sin\psi,\ v=-W\cos\psi$ ($\psi$ the direction the wind blows \emph{from}),
projects onto it as
\begin{equation}
    w_x = u\sin\chi + v\cos\chi = -W\cos(\psi-\chi), \qquad
    w_y = u\cos\chi - v\sin\chi = -W\sin(\psi-\chi),
\end{equation}
with $w_x$ the downrange component feeding $\vec{v}_w$ and $w_z=0$. The cross-plane component
$w_y$ is computed and discarded: crosswind weathercocking needs a full 6DOF model.

\paragraph{Analytic.} Power-law shear with constant direction:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:wind_shear}
    W(h) = W_{ref}\left(\max(h,h_{ref})/h_{ref}\right)^{n}, \qquad \psi = \psi_0.
\end{equation}
$h$ is the altitude above the local ground and $n\approx1/7$ the shear exponent (open terrain;
lower for smoother, up to $\sim0.4$ for rougher terrain). It is valid within the surface layer only
--- the lowest few hundred metres to 1--2\,km --- above which the sounding tiers apply. This is the
\emph{mean} profile; the fluctuation about it is in \autoref{sec:noise}.

\paragraph{Sounding / field.} The same measured $(u,v)$ profiles and interpolation as the
atmosphere, projected through the formula above; never interpolated as $(W,\psi)$, since direction
wraps at $360^\circ$.

\begin{aitext}
\subsection{Noise and disturbances}\label{sec:noise}
Two random effects perturb the flight about the deterministic models: the motor's thrust and the
air's motion. Both are optional and off by default.

\subsubsection{Thrust noise}
Even a perfectly aligned motor does not push exactly along its axis. The flow through the nozzle
produces a small, random side force, from jet and throat asymmetry, nozzle flow separation,
combustion instability and exhaust swirl, usually a small fraction of $T$. It is modelled along
the thrust-frame normal $\hat{z}_t$ (\autoref{eq:thrust_frame}),
\begin{equation}
    \vec{T}_s(t)=\kappa\,T(t)\,\xi(t)\,\hat{z}_t,
\end{equation}
with $\kappa$ the intensity as a fraction of thrust and $\xi(t)$ a zero-mean unit-variance random
process with a correlation time $\tau_c$. It enters the equations of motion as part of the thrust,
applied at $t$, so it also adds a moment about $g$. The same flow produces a motor roll torque
about $\hat{x}_r$ (internal vortex flow, nozzle ablation), out of the pitch plane and so without
effect in 2D. It is larger for star or finned grains, appears while the slotted part burns, and
has high peaks but small angular impulse; peaks up to $163\,\mathrm{N\,m}$ were measured on SCOUT
stages, and no prediction method is known \cite{knauberroll}.
\restodo{roll-torque magnitude and model for small motors: no $\kappa T\xi$-type model in the
literature}
For large solid motors the thrust-vector misalignment is quoted as a fraction of a degree
($0.1^\circ$ to $0.5^\circ$, i.e.\ $\kappa\approx\tan\delta\approx2\times10^{-3}$ to
$9\times10^{-3}$), and its time structure is a random, nearly constant bias plus short transients
at ignition and tail-off rather than a stationary process \cite{knauber}.

\subsubsection{Atmospheric disturbances}\label{sec:atmos_noise}
The mean wind of \autoref{sec:wind} is deterministic; turbulence is a stationary random
fluctuation about it, known only through its statistics (intensity $\sigma$, length scale $L$).
It perturbs the air's velocity, so it belongs to the wind. Two variants, selected independently of
the mean profile: \textbf{none} (default) and \textbf{Dryden}, the MIL-F-8785C
\cite{milf8785} continuous-turbulence spectrum, on the analytic tier only.
\simtodo{a discrete-gust model, as a deterministic wind increment $\Delta\vec{v}_w(t)$, is not
implemented}

The spectrum has three components: longitudinal $u$ (along the mean wind), lateral $v$ (horizontal,
across it) and vertical $w$. In the flight plane $w$ always acts and $u$ acts through its
projection; $v$ is out of plane when the mean wind lies in the plane, and is carried for the 3D
model.
\simtodo{only the horizontal $u$ and $v$ are implemented; the vertical component $w$, which is
in-plane, is missing. Low altitude, MIL-F-8785C: $L_w=h$, $\sigma_w=0.1W_{20}$, $\Phi_w$ of the form
of $\Phi_v$ with $(L_w,\sigma_w)$}
MIL-HDBK-1797 uses instead $L_w=h/2$, $L_v=L_u/2$ with a matching PSD form; the two conventions
must not be mixed, and this report keeps MIL-F-8785C.

The scales and intensities are the low-altitude formulas, in feet ($h_{ft}=h/0.3048$, $h$ the
altitude above ground, clamped at zero), with $W_{20}$ the mean profile \autoref{eq:wind_shear} at
$20\,\mathrm{ft}$:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dryden_scales}
    L_u = L_v = \frac{h_{ft}}{\left(0.177+0.000823\,h_{ft}\right)^{1.2}}, \quad L_w=h_{ft}, \qquad
    \sigma_w = 0.1\,W_{20}, \quad
    \sigma_u = \sigma_v = \frac{\sigma_w}{\left(0.177+0.000823\,h_{ft}\right)^{0.4}}.
\end{equation}
Three bands, the middle one an interpolation across the gap the spec leaves open:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l l l l}
Band & Condition & $L$ [ft] & $\sigma$ \\
low        & $h_{ft}\leq1000$      & \autoref{eq:dryden_scales} & \autoref{eq:dryden_scales} \\
transition & $1000<h_{ft}<2000$    & linear to $1750$ & linear to $\sigma_{high}$ \\
isotropic  & $h_{ft}\geq2000$      & $1750$, all components & $\sigma_{high}$ (an input) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The one-sided temporal power spectral densities, normalised to $\int_0^\infty\Phi\,\mathrm{d}\omega
=\sigma^2$, are
\begin{gather}
    \Phi_u(\omega) = \sigma_u^2\,\frac{2L_u}{\pi v_{ref}}\,\frac{1}{1+\left(L_u\omega/v_{ref}\right)^2}
        \label{eq:dryden_psd_u} \\
    \Phi_v(\omega) = \sigma_v^2\,\frac{L_v}{\pi v_{ref}}\,
        \frac{1+3\left(L_v\omega/v_{ref}\right)^2}{\left[1+\left(L_v\omega/v_{ref}\right)^2\right]^2}
        \label{eq:dryden_psd_v}
\end{gather}
with $v_{ref}$ a fixed reference airspeed. A realisation is a frozen random-phase sum over a
log-spaced grid $\{\omega_i\}$ (periods $600\,\mathrm{s}$ to $0.2\,\mathrm{s}$), with the
amplitudes renormalised so that $\mathrm{Var}[u]=\sigma_u^2$ exactly:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dryden_synthesis}
    k_i = \Phi(\omega_i)\Delta\omega_i, \qquad
    w_i = \sqrt{\frac{2k_i}{\sum_jk_j}}, \qquad
    u(t) = \sigma_u\sum_i w_i\sin(\omega_it+\varphi_i),
\end{equation}
and likewise for $v(t)$ with independent phases; the phases $\{\varphi_i\}$ are drawn once per seed.

Along the mean wind $u$ and across it $v$ compose with the flight-plane projection rather than
adding to $W$ as scalars. With $\hat{e}_u$ the unit vector along the mean flow, $\hat{e}_v$ it
rotated by $+90^\circ$ in the horizontal plane, and $\hat{y}_0$ the cross-plane axis,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dryden_composition}
    \hat{e}_u = -\cos(\psi-\chi)\,\hat{x}_0 - \sin(\psi-\chi)\,\hat{y}_0, \qquad
    \hat{e}_v = \sin(\psi-\chi)\,\hat{x}_0 - \cos(\psi-\chi)\,\hat{y}_0,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dryden_wind}
    (w_x,w_y) = (W+u)\,\hat{e}_u + v\,\hat{e}_v,
\end{equation}
which reduces to the mean-only projection when $u=v=0$ and is not clamped, so $W+u<0$ is a gust
reversing the local flow. The 2D closure discards $w_y$, so $v$ has no effect on the trajectory.
The perturbation is zero at $h=0$, where $L=0$.

Limitation: $v_{ref}$ is a fixed input, not the rocket's $v_{rel}$, since a spectrum driven by the
instantaneous airspeed would make the right-hand side depend on more than $(t,\vec{y})$ and break
the adaptive solver. It sets only where the spectrum's knee sits in frequency, not the intensity.

\begin{aitext}
\subsection{Non-inertial frame corrections (Earth's rotation)}\label{sec:noninertial}
The WGS84 gravity magnitude already contains the centrifugal effect of the Earth's rotation at the
launch site. Reinterpreting $\mathcal{F}_0$ as Earth-fixed adds two fictitious accelerations to the
translational equation:
\begin{equation}
    \vec{a}_{Coriolis} = -2\vec{\Omega}\times\vec{v}_r, \qquad
    \vec{a}_{centrifugal} = -\vec{\Omega}\times(\vec{\Omega}\times\vec{r}),
\end{equation}
with $\vec{\Omega}$ the Earth's angular velocity ($|\vec{\Omega}|=7.2921\times10^{-5}\,
\mathrm{rad/s}$) and $\vec{r},\vec{v}_r$ the rocket's position and velocity in the Earth-fixed
frame. The Coriolis term depends on the flight's own velocity and heading, and is the one
implemented.

\paragraph{Ground-frame basis.} Work in the local East--North--Up triad $(\hat{E},\hat{N},\hat{U})$
at the launch site $(\phi_0,\lambda_0)$, with $\phi_0$ the geodetic latitude (written $\phi$ in the
gravity model). $\mathcal{F}_0$ is fixed by the launch heading $\chi$ (degrees clockwise from
North, the direction of $+\hat{x}_0$) and $\hat{z}_0$ pointing \emph{down}:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{x}_0 = \sin\chi\,\hat{E} + \cos\chi\,\hat{N}, \qquad
    \hat{y}_0 = \cos\chi\,\hat{E} - \sin\chi\,\hat{N}, \qquad
    \hat{z}_0 = -\hat{U}.
\end{equation}
The triad is right-handed, $\hat{x}_0\times\hat{y}_0=\hat{z}_0$, and $\hat{y}_0$ points to the
\emph{right} of the downrange direction. The decomposition of $\vec{\Omega}$ below is evaluated
once, at the launch site, in this fixed frame; it does \emph{not} follow a rotating $\hat{z}_g$.

\paragraph{Components of $\vec{\Omega}$.} The rotation axis points to the celestial north pole,
which at latitude $\phi_0$ lies in the local meridian plane, tilted from the vertical by the
co-latitude:
\begin{equation}
    \vec{\Omega} = \Omega\left(\cos\phi_0\,\hat{N} + \sin\phi_0\,\hat{U}\right), \qquad
    \Omega = 7.2921\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{rad/s}.
\end{equation}
Projecting onto the basis vectors,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:omega_components}
    \vec{\Omega} = \Omega\left(\cos\phi_0\cos\chi\,\hat{x}_0
        - \cos\phi_0\sin\chi\,\hat{y}_0 - \sin\phi_0\,\hat{z}_0\right).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Which component matters in 2D.} With $\vec{v}_r=v_x\hat{x}_0+v_z\hat{z}_0$,
\begin{equation}
    \vec{\Omega}\times\vec{v}_r =
        \Omega_yv_z\,\hat{x}_0
        + \left(\Omega_zv_x - \Omega_xv_z\right)\hat{y}_0
        - \Omega_yv_x\,\hat{z}_0,
\end{equation}
so
\begin{equation} \label{eq:coriolis_split}
    \vec{a}_{Coriolis} = -2\vec{\Omega}\times\vec{v}_r =
        \underbrace{-2\Omega_y\left(v_z\hat{x}_0 - v_x\hat{z}_0\right)}_{\text{in-plane}}
        \;\underbrace{-\,2\left(\Omega_zv_x - \Omega_xv_z\right)\hat{y}_0}_{\text{out-of-plane}}.
\end{equation}
The in-plane acceleration comes \emph{entirely} from $\Omega_y$, the component normal to the
flight plane; $\Omega_x$ and $\Omega_z$, lying inside the plane, give a purely out-of-plane
acceleration. Projecting $\vec{\Omega}$ onto the plane would keep exactly the wrong half.

\paragraph{Simplification (2D closure).} Only the in-plane bracket is retained. This is not a
smallness argument: the discarded lateral acceleration is of the same order, and for some
geometries larger. It is dropped because the model has no lateral degree of freedom, the same
closure as for the crosswind $w_y$, lifted by the same future 6DOF work. Consequently
$\Omega_y=-\Omega\cos\phi_0\sin\chi$ vanishes for a launch due North or South and at the poles,
where the 2D model predicts \emph{zero} Coriolis effect although the true effect is maximal and
lateral. Substituting $\Omega_y$,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:coriolis_2d}
    \vec{a}_{Coriolis}^{\,2D} = 2\Omega\cos\phi_0\sin\chi\left(v_z\,\hat{x}_0 - v_x\,\hat{z}_0\right),
\end{equation}
that is, with $v_z<0$ for an ascending rocket,
\begin{equation}
    a_{Coriolis,x} = +2\Omega\cos\phi_0\sin\chi\;v_z, \qquad
    a_{Coriolis,z} = -2\Omega\cos\phi_0\sin\chi\;v_x.
\end{equation}
At the equator with $\hat{x}_0$ East this gives the westward drift of a rising rocket and the
E\"otv\"os lift of an eastward one.

\paragraph{Residual centrifugal term.} Write the geocentric position as $\vec{r}=\vec{R}_0+\vec{s}$,
with $\vec{R}_0$ from the Earth's centre to the launch site and $\vec{s}=x\hat{x}_0+z\hat{z}_0$ the
simulation's position. Since $-\vec{\Omega}\times(\vec{\Omega}\times\vec{a})=\Omega^2\vec{a}-
(\vec{\Omega}\cdot\vec{a})\vec{\Omega}$ is linear in $\vec{a}$,
\begin{equation}
    \vec{a}_{centrifugal} = \underbrace{\Omega^2\vec{R}_0-(\vec{\Omega}\cdot\vec{R}_0)\vec{\Omega}}
        _{\text{constant, already in }\gamma_0(\phi)}
        + \underbrace{\Omega^2\vec{s}-(\vec{\Omega}\cdot\vec{s})\vec{\Omega}}_{\text{residual}}.
\end{equation}
The residual is bounded by $\Omega^2|\vec{s}|=5.3\times10^{-9}\,|\vec{s}|$, i.e.\
$5\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ at $|\vec{s}|=10\,\mathrm{km}$, three orders below the Coriolis
term, and is left unimplemented.

\paragraph{Moment.} Both accelerations are body forces proportional to mass, so their resultant acts
at $g$ and gives \emph{zero} moment about it. The only coupling is a gyroscopic torque from the
body's pitch rate, of order $2I\Omega\omega_r$, i.e.\ about $1.5\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}$ at
$\omega_r=1\,\mathrm{rad/s}$; it is dropped.

\paragraph{Result.} The translational equation gains one term, evaluated directly in
$\mathcal{F}_0$ like $\vec{W}$:
\begin{equation}
    m\vec{a}_r = \vec{W} +\vec{T} + \vec{A}_f + m\,\vec{a}_{Coriolis}^{\,2D}.
\end{equation}
It adds no parameter beyond $\phi_0$ and $\chi$. At $|\vec{v}_r|=300\,\mathrm{m/s}$ it is at most
$4.4\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ (equatorial East launch), about $2.8\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$
at $\phi_0=50^\circ$: $0.3\%$ of $g$, or order $10\,\mathrm{m}$ of displacement over a $30\,
\mathrm{s}$ powered ascent. This is the same order as the $g\,x/R_e\approx1.5\times10^{-2}\,
\mathrm{m/s^2}$ (at $x=10\,\mathrm{km}$) that gravity acquires along the rotated vertical; the two
are \emph{independent} and must not be multiplied together.

\begin{aitext}
\subsection{States, initial conditions and integration}\label{sec:states}
The rocket has two groups of kinematic states, resolved in the fixed frame $\mathcal{F}_0$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{translation}: position $\vec{x}_r=x\,\hat{x}_0+z\,\hat{z}_0$, velocity
    $\vec{v}_r=v_x\,\hat{x}_0+v_z\,\hat{z}_0$ and acceleration $\vec{a}_r$;
    \item \emph{orientation}: pitch angle $\theta_r$, pitch rate $\omega_r$ and angular acceleration
    $\varepsilon_r$.
\end{itemize}
Each level is the time derivative of the one below it, and the mass follows the motor mass flow:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:state_kinematics}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}\vec{x}_r}{\mathrm{d}t}=\vec{v}_r, \quad
    \frac{\mathrm{d}\vec{v}_r}{\mathrm{d}t}=\vec{a}_r, \qquad
    \frac{\mathrm{d}\theta_r}{\mathrm{d}t}=\omega_r, \quad
    \frac{\mathrm{d}\omega_r}{\mathrm{d}t}=\varepsilon_r, \qquad
    \dot m=\dot m_m=-\frac{T_0(t)}{\mathrm{ISP}\,g_0}.
\end{equation}
The accelerations $\vec{a}_r$, $\varepsilon_r$ are outputs of \autoref{eq:eom_trans} and
\autoref{eq:eom_rot}, evaluated at every call of the right-hand side, not states. The integrated
state vector has dimension seven,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:state_vector}
    \vec{y} = \left(x,\ z,\ v_x,\ v_z,\ \theta_r,\ \omega_r,\ m\right),
\end{equation}
with $z$ and $v_z$ positive \emph{down}. $m$ is read only when the variable-mass model is on;
otherwise $m$ and $I$ are fixed and $\dot m=0$. The right-hand side has three modes, selected in
this order: on the rail, under canopy, otherwise free flight.
The mass flow is that of the specific-impulse model (\autoref{sec:mass_mass}).

\paragraph{Initial conditions.} The default start is at rest on the pad, at ignition, with the nose
along the rail:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:initial_conditions}
    \vec{x}_r(0)=\vec{0}, \quad \vec{v}_r(0)=\vec{0}, \quad
    \theta_r(0)=\theta_{rail}, \quad \omega_r(0)=0, \quad m(0)=m_{launch},
\end{equation}
with $\theta_{rail}$ the rail elevation (default $90^\circ$) and $m_{launch}=m_r+m_0$ the liftoff
mass. Time starts at $t=0$, the ignition instant of the thrust curve. The launch site supplies
\begin{itemize}
    \item latitude $\phi_0$ and heading $\chi$ --- Earth-rotation correction and ellipsoidal gravity;
    \item elevation $h_0$ --- atmosphere and gravity only; it never enters $z$;
    \item rail elevation and length --- the launch rail.
\end{itemize}
A scenario may override the whole state; nothing checks that $\theta_r(0)=\theta_{rail}$. The
models carry no hidden dynamical state, so they are pure functions of $(t,\vec{y})$.

\paragraph{Integrators.} Two integrators are available behind one interface, chosen by a setting:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adaptive Runge--Kutta 4(5)} (default): the \textsc{scipy} \texttt{RK45} scheme
    with relative tolerance $10^{-8}$ and absolute tolerance $10^{-10}$ on every component of
    $\vec{y}$, dense output on, and a time limit of $300\,\mathrm{s}$. Events are located to solver
    tolerance by root finding on the dense output.
    \item \textbf{Fixed-step classical Runge--Kutta 4}, hand-written, with step $h$ (default
    $1\,\mathrm{ms}$):
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:rk4}
        \vec{y}_{k+1}=\vec{y}_k+\frac{h}{6}\left(\vec{k}_1+2\vec{k}_2+2\vec{k}_3+\vec{k}_4\right),
    \end{equation}
    with $\vec{k}_1=\vec{f}(t_k,\vec{y}_k)$, $\vec{k}_{2,3}=\vec{f}\!\left(t_k+\tfrac{h}{2},
    \vec{y}_k+\tfrac{h}{2}\vec{k}_{1,2}\right)$, $\vec{k}_4=\vec{f}(t_k+h,
    \vec{y}_k+h\,\vec{k}_3)$ and $\vec{f}$ the right-hand side. It has no step control; events are
    detected afterwards from sign changes between steps and placed by linear interpolation, so they
    are accurate to about $h$. It is the cross-check of the adaptive path and the only one that
    supports the sampled-data mode.
\end{itemize}
Both integrate the same right-hand side and differ only in numerical error.

\paragraph{Events.} Five events are watched; only the first ends the run. Each is a sign change of
a scalar function of $(t,\vec{y})$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ground impact} (terminal): the ground depth crosses zero going down, $z$ for flat
    ground, the curved-ground depth otherwise. Ignored for the first $0.1\,\mathrm{s}$, since
    $z=0$ at the start;
    \item \textbf{apogee}: $v_z$ crosses from negative to positive in the launch frame, also
    ignored for the first $0.1\,\mathrm{s}$;
    \item \textbf{burnout}: the thrust falls through a small threshold ($10^{-9}$\,N) from above,
    so a motor that never produces thrust never triggers it;
    \item \textbf{rail exit}: $s_{rail}$ crosses $L_{rail}$ upwards; never active when
    $L_{rail}=0$, and also ignored for the first $0.1\,\mathrm{s}$;
    \item \textbf{parachute deployment}: the deployment predicate turns true; never active without
    a recovery model.
\end{itemize}

\begin{aitext}
\subsection{Sampled-data evaluation (zero-order hold)}\label{sec:sampled}
This is an interchangeable \emph{mode of evaluating} the models, changing \emph{when} a quantity is
computed, not \emph{what} it is. By default every model is a pure function of $(t,\vec{y})$
(\autoref{eq:state_vector}), re-evaluated at every right-hand-side call. A flight computer's
measure--compute--hold cycle, an integral term or a filter with internal state cannot be expressed
that way; this mode makes it expressible.

With a sample rate $f_s>0$ and period $\tau_s=1/f_s$, the ticks are $t_k=k\tau_s$. At each tick every
registered \emph{channel} --- a named sampled quantity --- is evaluated once, at that tick's state,
and held:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:zoh}
    c(t) = c_k \equiv c\!\left(t_k,\vec{y}(t_k);\,c_{k-1}\right), \qquad t\in[t_k,t_{k+1}).
\end{equation}
A channel receives \emph{its own} previous held value $c_{k-1}$ (undefined at the first tick). That
makes it recursive, and so an integral accumulator, a rate limiter's previous command or a discrete
filter state are each a function of this form. One channel exists, the deployment fraction $f_b$;
its laws are memoryless and ignore $c_{k-1}$, so the mode currently changes only \emph{how often}
$f_b$ is computed.

Three requirements follow from \autoref{eq:zoh}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Fixed-step integration, with $\tau_s$ an exact integer multiple of the step.} An
    adaptive step cannot honour a hold boundary, and $\tau_s/h$ must be an integer so that every
    boundary falls on a step boundary. A non-integer ratio is rejected rather than rounded, which
    would silently change the sample rate.
    \item \textbf{The hold is constant across every stage of a substep.} The right-hand side is then
    smooth inside a tick and the integrator's order is preserved; every discontinuity lands on a
    step boundary.
    \item \textbf{The sample is taken at the tick's start}, $(t_k,\vec{y}(t_k))$: measure, compute,
    apply until the next tick, the causal order of a flight computer.
\end{enumerate}
Two properties of a sampled run are not defects. It is a \emph{different dynamical system} from the
continuous one, not a more accurate solution of it; the meaningful comparison is ``this actuator at
this sample rate'' against ``an ideal continuous actuator''. And it is substantially more expensive
(small fixed steps for the whole flight), which is why it is opt-in: $f_s=0$ is the default, and
the mode is then structurally absent. It supplies the place to run a controller with memory, and
nothing else: no such controller and no state-coupled turbulence model is written here.
